## Why Your Graded Homework Just Became Optional (And How to Adapt)

Fields Medalist Richard Borcherds, a mathematics professor at UC Berkeley, has officially stopped assigning graded homework. His reasoning is blunt: grading AI-generated answers is a waste of time and money. Instead, he now relies on in-person exams to measure what students actually know. If you're a student reading this, the takeaway is immediate and uncomfortable — **the safety net of "points for completion" is disappearing**, and your grade will hinge almost entirely on closed-book, in-person performance. This guide gives you a concrete study system to survive that shift, plus a comparison of the tools and tactics that still work when AI can't carry you.

The core problem isn't that AI exists. It's that AI made *take-home work unverifiable*. When a professor can't tell whether you or a chatbot solved problem 14, the only trustworthy signal left is a proctored exam. Your job is to build the skills that survive that filter.

## Step-by-Step: Building an AI-Proof Study System

Here's the practical workflow to replace graded homework with self-verification.

### Step 1: Convert Homework Into Self-Testing
Treat every ungraded problem set as a mock exam. Set a timer, close all tabs, and solve on paper. Grade yourself against the solution key *after* the timer ends.

### Step 2: Use AI as a Tutor, Not an Answer Machine
The productive use of AI is Socratic, not generative. Compare the two modes:

| AI Usage Mode | What You Do | Learning Value | Exam Transfer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answer generation | Paste problem, copy output | Near zero | Fails in-person |
| Socratic tutoring | Ask "why is step 2 valid?" | High | Transfers well |
| Error diagnosis | Submit your attempt, ask for the flaw | Very high | Transfers well |
| Concept drilling | Request 10 variations of a problem | High | Builds fluency |

### Step 3: Build a Closed-Book Retrieval Habit
In-person exams test *retrieval under pressure*. Practice it weekly:
1. Write a one-page "cheat sheet" from memory, then check it against notes.
2. Redo the three hardest problems from each unit with no references.
3. Log every concept you blanked on — that's your real study list.

### Step 4: Recreate Exam Conditions
Borcherds-style exams reward fluency, not lookup. Simulate:
- **No notes, no calculator** unless the syllabus permits it.
- **Fixed time blocks** matching the real exam length.
- **Handwritten work** if your course still uses blue books or paper exams.

### Step 5: Track Mastery, Not Completion
Since completion points are gone, build your own metrics. A simple weekly scorecard:

- Problems solved cold (no hints): __ / 10
- Concepts recalled from memory: __ / 10
- Errors diagnosed and fixed: __ / 5
- Time per problem vs. exam pace: __ min

## What You Should Know: Risks, Workarounds, and Alternatives

**The grading gap is real.** Without graded homework, your only feedback loop is self-generated. That means you must be honest with yourself — the students who fail this transition are the ones who *stop doing problems entirely* because "they don't count."

**Not every course will follow.** Borcherds' move is a signal, not a universal policy. Many departments are pivoting to in-person quizzes, oral exams, or AI-resistant problem design instead of dropping homework. Check your syllabus early and ask your instructor directly how grades are weighted.

**Oral and in-person components are rising.** Expect more weight on:
- In-class quizzes and midterms
- Oral defenses or "explain your work" sessions
- Proctored, browser-locked exams

**Cost angle:** Borcherds frames grading AI answers as wasted money — TA hours spent reading machine output. For you, the practical cost is *time*: you now have to grade yourself. Budget 20–30 minutes per problem set for self-assessment.

**Tool alternatives that still help:**
- Spaced-repetition apps (Anki) for formula and definition recall.
- Paper-only problem journals to force retrieval.
- Study groups where each person explains a solution aloud — mimics oral exams.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Why did the UC Berkeley professor stop grading homework?
Richard Borcherds said grading AI-generated answers wastes time and money, so he shifted to in-person exams to accurately measure student understanding.

### Will other professors stop grading homework too?
Some will, especially in large math and STEM courses where AI detection is unreliable. Others will keep homework but move assessment weight to proctored, in-person formats.

### How do I study if homework isn't graded?
Self-test under timed, closed-book conditions, use AI only for Socratic questioning and error diagnosis, and track mastery with a weekly scorecard instead of completion points.

### Does using AI to study count as cheating?
Using AI to *understand* concepts is generally fine; submitting AI-generated answers as your own work is academic dishonesty. The line is whether the output is your demonstrated skill.

## Final Verdict

Borcherds is right about the economics: grading machine output is a losing game, and in-person exams are the honest filter. For students, the smart move is to **stop optimizing for completion and start optimizing for retrieval**. Build a closed-book self-testing routine now, use AI as a tutor rather than a crutch, and treat every ungraded problem set as a free practice exam. The students who adapt fastest to the in-person-only era will be the ones who were already testing themselves — not the ones waiting for points.
