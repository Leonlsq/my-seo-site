## Is the InfinaKore Fender Telecaster Controller Worth It for Stage Tour?

The InfinaKore Fender Telecaster Edition is a modular rhythm-game guitar controller built for the new **Stage Tour** plus legacy **Guitar Hero** and **Rock Band** titles — and its headline feature is that you can 3D-print and swap your own parts. Short answer: **it's worth it if you already own a 3D printer and play multiple rhythm games**; if you only play Stage Tour casually, a stock controller is the cheaper path. This guide breaks down the specs, the real cost of 3D-printed customization, and the setup steps so you can decide before you buy.

## What You Get: InfinaKore Fender Telecaster Specs vs. Stock Controllers

The controller ships as a semi-assembled platform with a printed shell, a swappable strum bar, fret buttons, and a mechanical d-pad/whammy base. The key differentiator is the **STL/STEP file library** InfinaKore releases for the body, fret caps, strum bar, and grip panels.

| Feature | InfinaKore Telecaster | Standard Stage Tour Controller | Legacy GH/RB Guitar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Console/PC compatibility | Stage Tour, GH, Rock Band (multi-platform) | Stage Tour only | Title-specific |
| 3D-printable parts | Body, fret caps, strum bar, grips | No | No |
| Fret button type | Mechanical, swappable caps | Membrane | Membrane |
| Strum bar | Replaceable module | Fixed | Fixed |
| Whammy | Hall-effect option | Analog pot | Analog pot |
| Repairability | High (print replacements) | Low | Low |
| Price (approx.) | $150–$200 | $80–$110 | $60–$120 used |

**Bottom line on specs:** you're paying a premium for modularity and repairability, not raw performance. The mechanical frets and hall-effect whammy are genuine upgrades, but the 3D-print ecosystem is the actual selling point.

## Step-by-Step: How to 3D-Print and Swap Parts on the Telecaster Controller

Here's the practical workflow most buyers will actually use.

1. **Download the official part library.** Grab the STL/STEP files from InfinaKore's support page. STEP files matter if you plan to remix in Fusion 360 or FreeCAD.
2. **Check your printer's build volume.** The full body shell typically needs a **220 × 220 × 250 mm** bed or larger. If your printer is smaller, print the split-body variant and join with the provided dovetail clips.
3. **Pick the right filament.** Use **PETG or ABS/ASA** for the strum bar and fret caps — PLA is brittle and will crack under repeated strumming. Use PLA only for cosmetic grip panels.
4. **Print at the recommended settings.** InfinaKore's docs suggest **0.2 mm layer height, 3–4 perimeters, and 25–35% infill** for structural parts. Higher infill adds weight without much strength gain.
5. **Test-fit before final assembly.** Fret caps snap onto the mechanical switches; if they're tight, sand the mating edge lightly rather than forcing them.
6. **Calibrate the strum bar.** After swapping, run the in-game calibration (Stage Tour has a dedicated latency test) to confirm the strum module registers both up and down strokes.
7. **Remix responsibly.** If you tweak geometry, keep the switch mounting points unchanged — those tolerances are the one thing you shouldn't mess with.

## Common Issues, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

**Print quality affects feel, not just looks.** A rough strum bar with visible layer lines will feel gritty. Print strum bars vertically or post-process with light sanding.

**Filament choice changes acoustics.** Dense PETG gives a heavier, quieter strum; PLA feels clickier but wears faster.

**Cost reality check.** Filament for a full custom shell runs roughly **$8–$15**, plus electricity and print time (10–20 hours for a full body). That's cheap — but only if you already own a printer. Buying a printer just for this is not economical.

**Alternatives to consider:**
- **Stock Stage Tour controller** — cheapest, zero setup, no customization.
- **Legacy Guitar Hero/Rock Band guitars** — abundant used, but aging membranes and no Stage Tour guarantee.
- **Other modular controllers** — a few competitors offer swappable frets, but none match the open file library here.

**Compatibility caveat:** confirm your platform (PC, console generation) against InfinaKore's compatibility chart before ordering — multi-game support varies by adapter/firmware.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Is the InfinaKore Fender Telecaster controller compatible with Guitar Hero and Rock Band?
Yes — InfinaKore markets it as compatible with the new Stage Tour plus pre-existing Guitar Hero and Rock Band games. Verify your specific platform and game version against their compatibility chart, since adapters and firmware can affect support.

### Do I need a 3D printer to use the InfinaKore controller?
No. It ships with a printed shell and works out of the box. The 3D printer is only needed if you want to customize, replace, or repair parts using the official STL/STEP files.

### What filament should I use for 3D-printed guitar controller parts?
Use **PETG or ABS/ASA** for structural parts like the strum bar and fret caps. Reserve PLA for cosmetic panels, since it's more brittle under repeated stress.

### How much does it cost to 3D-print custom parts for the controller?
Filament for a full custom shell typically costs **$8–$15**, plus 10–20 hours of print time. The controller itself runs roughly $150–$200.

## Final Verdict

**Buy the InfinaKore Fender Telecaster Edition if** you own a 3D printer, play Stage Tour alongside legacy Guitar Hero/Rock Band, and value repairability and mechanical frets. The open part library is a genuine long-term advantage — you can replace a cracked strum bar for pennies instead of buying a new guitar.

**Skip it if** you're a casual Stage Tour player or don't own a printer. A stock controller at $80–$110 delivers the same gameplay without the setup overhead. The Telecaster is a platform for tinkerers, and it earns its price only when you actually use the customization it's built around.
