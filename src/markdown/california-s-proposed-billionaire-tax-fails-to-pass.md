## What Prop 40's Failure Actually Means for Your Tech Equity and Tax Plan

California voters rejected Proposition 40, the one-time 5% tax on billionaires, on Monday per the AP — and tech leaders outspent backers roughly 10 to 1 to kill it. If you're a founder, early employee, or RSU holder, the practical takeaway is simple: **no new wealth tax is coming in the 2026 tax year, but the residency and diversification questions the campaign raised are still live.** This guide turns the headline into an actionable checklist for anyone holding concentrated California-based equity.

## Step-by-Step: Rebuilding Your Tax and Equity Plan After Prop 40

The proposition's defeat removes one variable but changes nothing about federal capital gains, California's top marginal rate, or the exit-tax proposals that keep resurfacing in Sacramento. Work through these steps in order.

1. **Confirm your actual exposure.** Prop 40 targeted a one-time 5% levy on net worth above a high threshold (reported around the billionaire mark). If your paper net worth sits well below that line, your planning priority is ordinary capital gains and AMT, not wealth tax. Pull your latest 409A valuation and brokerage statements to get a real number.
2. **Map your equity vesting against the 2026–2027 calendar.** A failed ballot measure does not stop future versions from appearing. If a large RSU or option tranche vests after a potential 2028 ballot cycle, model the after-tax outcome under both "no new tax" and "5% wealth tax" scenarios.
3. **Audit your California residency documentation.** The founders TechCrunch interviewed were split on whether the tax would have driven wealthy residents out. Regardless of outcome, if you're considering a move to Texas, Nevada, or Washington, start the paper trail now: driver's license, voter registration, day-count logs, and lease or deed records.
4. **Rebalance concentration risk.** Tech leaders spent heavily to defeat Prop 40, which signals how much founders fear liquidity-triggering taxes. Use the reprieve to set a 10b5-1 plan or staged diversification schedule rather than waiting for a forced sale event.
5. **Re-run your exit model.** Update your cap table projections with the assumption that California's tax regime stays status quo through 2027, then stress-test for a reintroduced measure.

## Common Issues, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

- **"Does this mean I can ignore wealth-tax risk?"** No. A single failed proposition doesn't retire the idea. Track California legislative bills and any 2028 signature drives.
- **"Should I move out of state now?"** Only with clean residency evidence. California's Franchise Tax Board audits high-income departures aggressively; a move without documentation can trigger back taxes.
- **"What about my startup's Delaware C-corp?"** Entity structure doesn't shield personal residency-based taxation. Your personal domicile drives the analysis.
- **Alternative strategy:** If you're a founder with illiquid paper wealth, consider a secondary sale or tender offer to diversify before any future ballot measure forces the issue.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Did California's billionaire tax pass?
No. Voters rejected Proposition 40, the one-time 5% tax on billionaires, according to the AP. Tech leaders outspent supporters roughly 10 to 1 to defeat it.

### What happens to California taxes now that Prop 40 failed?
The status quo holds: California's top marginal rate stays at 13.3% plus the 1% mental health surtax above $1 million, and federal capital gains rules are unaffected.

### Will a billionaire tax come back in California?
Likely as a future ballot or legislative proposal. The 10:1 spending gap shows both sides are organized, so monitor 2028 signature drives and Sacramento bills.

### Should tech founders move out of California after Prop 40 failed?
Not automatically. Relocation only pays off with documented residency change and a clear comparison of state income tax, cost of living, and equity vesting timelines.

## Final Verdict

Prop 40's failure is a **stay of execution, not a pardon** for concentrated California equity holders. Don't celebrate by doing nothing — use the window to document residency, diversify holdings, and model a future wealth-tax scenario. Founders who treat this as a green light to stay fully concentrated are the ones most exposed if the measure returns in 2028.
