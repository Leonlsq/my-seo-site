## Why Is "Why Are We Even Doing All This?" Suddenly Everywhere?

If your developer feed is suddenly full of existential dread, you are not imagining it. A recent essay by mathematician Terence Tao titled "Why are we even doing all this?" hit the front page of Hacker News, racking up 177 points and 146 comments in a matter of hours. The post struck a nerve because it names a feeling most engineers, gamers, and creators have been quietly suppressing for years: **the gap between the effort we pour into technical work and the meaning we get back from it.**

This is not just a philosophical musing. It is a **tech burnout troubleshooting guide**. If you are reading this because you typed "why are we even doing all this" into Google at 2 AM after another sprint, this article gives you a concrete framework to diagnose the feeling, audit your actual workload, and decide whether to optimize, pivot, or walk away. Let's get into it.

## Step-by-Step: Auditing Your Tech Burnout (The 5-Point Framework)

Before you make any career or project decisions, run this audit. It takes about 30 minutes and mirrors the kind of structured reflection Tao's essay and the HN thread both point toward.

### Step 1: Separate the Three Types of "Why"
Not all existential dread is the same. Categorize it:

| Type | Symptom | Root Cause | Fix |
|------|---------|------------|-----|
| **Task-level** | "Why am I fixing this bug?" | Low autonomy, repetitive work | Automate or delegate |
| **Project-level** | "Why does this product exist?" | Misaligned goals, feature bloat | Re-scope or exit |
| **Career-level** | "Why am I in tech at all?" | Values mismatch, sunk cost | Long-term pivot plan |

Most people conflate all three. Naming which one you actually feel cuts the problem in half.

### Step 2: Run the Effort-to-Impact Ratio
For each major task on your plate this week, score it 1–5 on **effort** and 1–5 on **impact**. Anything scoring 4+ effort and 1–2 impact is a candidate for elimination. In the HN discussion, several commenters noted that the "why" question usually appears when this ratio silently drifts below 1:1.

### Step 3: Check Your Inputs, Not Just Your Outputs
Burnout is often a **signal problem**, not a motivation problem. Audit:
- **Sleep:** Under 6.5 hours for two weeks straight? Fix this first.
- **Context switching:** More than 4 concurrent projects? Consolidate.
- **Feedback loops:** No shipped work in 30+ days? You have lost the dopamine loop that sustains technical work.

### Step 4: Apply the "Would I Do This for Free?" Test (With a Caveat)
This is the classic test, but it is misused. You should not literally work for free. Instead ask: **"If money were equal, would I choose this project over the alternative?"** If the answer is consistently no, you have found the source.

### Step 5: Set a 90-Day Decision Deadline
Do not let the question float forever. Pick a date, and by that date commit to one of three actions: **optimize** (change role, team, or scope), **pivot** (move to a new domain), or **accept** (reframe the work as a means to a non-work end).

## Common Traps and What You Should Know

The Hacker News thread surfaced several recurring traps that make the "why" question worse rather than better:

- **The Sunk Cost Spiral:** "I have 10 years in this stack, I can't leave." You can. Skills transfer more than you think.
- **The Prestige Trap:** Chasing FAANG or trending frameworks because peers do. Prestige does not survive contact with a bad manager.
- **The Infinite Optimization Loop:** Endlessly tweaking your setup, dotfiles, or productivity system instead of addressing the actual work. This is procrastination in a hoodie.
- **The Comparison Tax:** Reading Hacker News or X all day and measuring your worst day against someone's highlight reel.

**Information gain worth noting:** Tao's essay is not a manifesto for quitting tech. It is a prompt to check whether your *current* activity matches your *stated* values. The gap between those two is where burnout lives.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Why is "Why are we even doing all this?" trending on Hacker News?
A blog post by Terence Tao with that title hit the Hacker News front page, generating 177 points and 146 comments. The discussion resonated because it captures a widespread feeling in the tech community about meaning, effort, and the sustainability of modern engineering work.

### Is tech burnout a real medical condition?
Burnout is recognized by the World Health Organization as an occupational phenomenon, not a classified medical condition. However, chronic burnout is linked to anxiety and depression, so if symptoms persist beyond a few weeks, consult a professional rather than relying on self-help frameworks alone.

### How do I know if I should quit my tech job or just change teams?
If the "why" feeling disappears on vacation but returns within 48 hours of logging back in, the problem is likely your team or manager, not the industry. If it persists even during time off, the issue is deeper and may warrant a career-level pivot.

## Final Verdict

The "why are we even doing all this" question is not a bug in your brain — it is a **diagnostic signal**. Do not suppress it, and do not let it spiral into paralysis. Run the 5-point audit above, set a 90-day deadline, and pick one of the three paths: optimize, pivot, or accept. The worst outcome is not choosing wrong; it is refusing to choose at all and letting the question quietly run your life for another five years. If you are going to do all this, at least know why.
