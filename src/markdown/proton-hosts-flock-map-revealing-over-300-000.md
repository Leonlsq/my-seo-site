## Proton Flock Map: How to Check If Your Camera Was Exposed

If you've heard that Proton AG is now hosting the Flock camera map—the crowdsourced surveillance map that a security researcher published last month—you're probably wondering one thing: **is my neighborhood on it, and is my own camera exposed?** The short answer: yes, you can check in under two minutes, and the map covers over 300,000 Flock Safety cameras across the United States. This guide walks you through exactly how to access the map, verify your own devices, and take practical steps if your camera or street shows up.

The backstory matters for context, but only briefly: a researcher published a map of Flock's automated license plate reader (ALPR) cameras, Flock sent cease-and-desist threats, and Proton—the Swiss privacy company behind Proton Mail and Proton VPN—stepped in to host a mirror. For you, the practical question isn't the legal drama. It's: **what do I do now that this data is public?**

## Step-by-Step: How to Check the Flock Map and Your Exposure

Here's the actionable part. Follow these steps in order.

### Step 1: Access the hosted map
1. Go to Proton's hosted mirror (linked from Proton's official blog and social channels—avoid random third-party copies, which may be altered or malicious).
2. Load the map in a privacy-focused browser (Firefox, Brave, or a hardened Chrome profile).
3. Zoom to your city, then your neighborhood.

### Step 2: Identify camera types near you
The map distinguishes between camera categories. Note what's near you:

| Camera Type | What It Captures | Privacy Risk Level |
|---|---|---|
| ALPR (license plate readers) | Plate numbers, timestamps, vehicle make/model | High — creates movement logs |
| Fixed surveillance | Continuous video feeds | Medium–High |
| Neighborhood/community cams | Shared residential feeds | Medium |

### Step 3: Cross-check against your own devices
- If you own a Flock camera (some HOAs and neighborhoods deploy them), verify its location pin matches reality.
- If you own a **different** brand (Ring, Nest, Wyze, Reolink), confirm it is *not* mislabeled on the map. False positives happen in crowdsourced datasets.

### Step 4: Document and screenshot
Take dated screenshots of any pin near your home. If you later pursue an opt-out or HOA complaint, this is your evidence trail.

### Step 5: Check your HOA or city contract
Call your HOA board or city clerk and ask directly: *"Do we have a Flock Safety contract, and what is the data-sharing policy?"* Many residents discover deployments they never voted on.

## Common Issues, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

### "The map won't load"
The mirror can get hammered after media coverage. Workarounds:
- Try a different browser or disable aggressive tracker blockers temporarily.
- Use Proton VPN to a nearby server if your ISP throttles the domain.
- Wait 24–48 hours; mirrors stabilize as traffic normalizes.

### "My camera is on the map but I never consented"
This is the most common complaint. Options:
1. **Contact Flock directly** and request removal—cite your state's privacy statutes.
2. **File a complaint with your state attorney general** if the deployment violates local surveillance ordinances.
3. **Push your HOA** to disclose the contract terms; many agreements include data-retention clauses residents never saw.

### Alternatives if you want to opt out of ALPR exposure
- **License plate covers** (check legality in your state—some ban them).
- **Route variation**: ALPRs log fixed choke points; varying routes reduces pattern data.
- **Support municipal transparency laws** requiring public ALPR registries.

### Is Proton hosting this legal?
Proton is hosting *publicly available* aggregated data as a free-speech and transparency stance. It is not hosting private feeds. Still, treat any mirror as potentially outdated—camera counts and pins shift as deployments change.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Is the Proton Flock map free to access?
Yes. Proton hosts the mirror at no cost as a transparency initiative. No account is required, though using Proton VPN or a privacy browser is recommended.

### How many cameras are on the Flock map?
The map covers **over 300,000 Flock Safety cameras** nationwide, based on the researcher's original dataset. Counts update as new deployments are reported.

### Can I get my camera removed from the map?
You can request removal from Flock and your deploying authority (HOA or city). Removal from a third-party mirror like Proton's is harder, since it reflects public records and crowdsourced reports.

### Does the map show live footage?
No. It shows **camera locations and metadata**, not live video feeds. The privacy concern is location and movement-logging, not real-time spying.

## Final Verdict

Don't panic—but do check. Spend five minutes on the Proton-hosted map, screenshot anything near your home, and make one phone call to your HOA or city clerk. If you find a deployment you never approved, document it and escalate through your attorney general or local transparency channels. The Flock map controversy is really a wake-up call about **surveillance you didn't know you had**—and the best defense is knowing exactly where those cameras are before someone else maps them for you.
