## Screwworm in Pets: What Pet Owners Actually Need to Do Right Now

The New World screwworm—a parasitic fly whose larvae burrow into living flesh—has now been confirmed in a third U.S. state, and pets are among the victims. The USDA has identified **74 animal cases nationwide since the invasion began in June**, and the spread shows no sign of stopping. If you own a dog, cat, or livestock animal, the question isn't whether this is scary—it's what you should physically do this week to keep your pet safe. This guide gives you the concrete warning signs, a wound-inspection routine, and a prevention checklist you can start today. The short version: **check every wound daily, keep pets indoors during peak fly hours, and call your vet the moment you see a moving wound.**

## The 7-Step Screwworm Prevention & Detection Routine

Screwworm isn't a "wait and see" condition. Larvae can hatch within 12–24 hours of eggs being laid, and untreated infestations can become life-threatening in days. Here's the routine to run:

1. **Inspect every wound, no matter how small.** Even a minor scratch, hot spot, or tick bite can attract a gravid female fly. Do a full-body check on your pet **once daily**—especially ears, nose, gums, paws, and the umbilical area on puppies.
2. **Know the tell-tale signs.** Unlike maggots that eat dead tissue, screwworm larvae feed on *living* flesh. Watch for:
   - A wound that **enlarges instead of healing**
   - **Serosanguinous (bloody, watery) discharge** with a foul smell
   - Visible **moving larvae** (cream-colored, screw-thread ridges, ~1–2 cm)
   - Pet **licking or biting obsessively** at one spot
   - Restlessness, reduced appetite, or lethargy
3. **Keep pets indoors during peak fly activity.** Screwworm flies are most active in **warm daylight hours (roughly 9 a.m.–5 p.m.)**. Close window screens—standard mesh blocks most flies.
4. **Treat wounds promptly and cover them.** Clean minor wounds with saline, apply a vet-recommended antiseptic, and use a **pet-safe wound cover or cone** to stop flies from landing.
5. **Control fly populations around your property.** Remove rotting organic matter, secure trash, and use fly traps. Screwworm flies are attracted to *open wounds*, not garbage—but reducing overall fly pressure helps.
6. **Check pets returning from travel.** If your pet has been to an affected state or near livestock in the last 14 days, do a **twice-daily** inspection for a week.
7. **Call your vet immediately if you suspect infestation.** Do **not** try to remove larvae yourself—squeezing them can cause tissue damage and secondary infection. Your vet will debride and treat properly.

## Common Issues, Regional Risk & What You Should Know

**Which states are affected?** As of this reporting, cases have been confirmed in three states following the June invasion, with the USDA tracking 74 animal cases. Because screwworm is a **reportable disease**, any confirmed case triggers federal response—so local alerts move fast. Check your state agriculture department's page weekly.

**Is it contagious between pets?** No—screwworm is spread by flies, not animal-to-animal contact. But an infested pet *attracts more flies*, so isolate and treat quickly.

**Cost reality check:** A simple wound check at the vet runs **$50–$150**. An established infestation requiring debridement, antibiotics, and hospitalization can run **$800–$3,000+**. Daily inspection is the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy.

**What NOT to do:**
- Don't use human insect repellents (DEET is toxic to pets).
- Don't apply petroleum jelly or home remedies to "smother" larvae—this can worsen tissue damage.
- Don't assume a "healing" wound is safe; screwworm wounds can look clean at the surface while larvae burrow deeper.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What are the first signs of screwworm in a dog or cat?
The earliest sign is a **small wound that gets worse instead of better**, often with bloody discharge and a foul odor. Your pet may lick or bite at the spot obsessively. Within a day or two, you may see cream-colored larvae with a screw-like ridged body. Any wound that changes for the worse within 24–48 hours warrants an immediate vet call.

### Can screwworm affect indoor-only pets?
Yes. Flies enter through open doors and windows, and even a tiny scratch or hot spot can attract them. Indoor pets are lower risk but **not zero risk**—daily wound checks still apply, especially in affected states.

### Is there a vaccine or preventive medication for screwworm in pets?
There is **no approved vaccine or oral preventive** for screwworm in companion animals. Prevention relies entirely on **wound hygiene, fly control, and indoor management**. For livestock, USDA-approved treatments exist, but pet owners should follow vet guidance only.

### How fast does screwworm spread once a pet is infested?
Larvae can hatch within **12–24 hours** of egg-laying and grow rapidly, causing significant tissue damage within **2–4 days**. This is why daily—sometimes twice-daily—inspection is critical during active fly season.

## Final Verdict

Screwworm is rare but genuinely dangerous, and the current three-state spread means **every pet owner in or near affected regions should treat daily wound checks as non-negotiable**. You don't need special gear—just a flashlight, five minutes a day, and a phone number for your vet on speed dial. If you see a wound that's getting worse, smells foul, or has visible larvae, **skip the internet and call your vet immediately**. Prevention is boring, cheap, and effective; treatment is expensive and stressful. Do the boring thing.
