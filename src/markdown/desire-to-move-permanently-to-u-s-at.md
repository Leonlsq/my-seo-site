## What the Gallup 'Desire to Move to U.S.' New Low Actually Means

Gallup's latest global migration poll just recorded the lowest share of adults worldwide who say they'd like to move permanently to the United States since the question was first tracked. The headline number is small, but the story behind it is not: the drop is concentrated in specific regions, driven by shifting economic opportunity and changing perceptions, and it has real, practical implications if you're one of the millions still planning a U.S. move. This guide breaks down the data, tells you which countries still show strong intent, and gives you a concrete planning framework so you're not reacting to a headline that doesn't apply to your situation.

The short version: **a lower global average does not mean the U.S. stopped being a top destination.** It means the *pool* of people who say "yes" to a hypothetical question shrank. Your actual visa category, country of origin, and profession matter far more than the global trend line.

## The Data Breakdown: Who Still Wants In and Who Doesn't

Gallup's methodology asks adults in 100+ countries whether they'd like to move permanently to another country, and if so, where. The "desire to migrate to the U.S." figure is a subset of that. Here's how to read the 2026 release against the trend:

| Metric | What It Measures | Why It Matters to You |
|---|---|---|
| Global desire to migrate (anywhere) | Share of adults wanting to leave their country | Sets the baseline pool |
| Desire to migrate to the U.S. | Share naming the U.S. as preferred destination | The headline number |
| Regional breakdown | Intent by region (LatAm, Sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, Europe) | Tells you where competition for visas is rising or falling |
| Demographic split | Age, education, income of respondents | Predicts who shows up in employment-based queues |

Key takeaways from the release:

- **The decline is not uniform.** Interest from Latin America and Sub-Saharan Africa has historically run far above the global average; a global "new low" often masks flat or rising intent in those regions.
- **Europe and high-income Asia drag the average down.** Falling intent there reflects strong domestic labor markets more than any change in U.S. policy.
- **The question is hypothetical.** "Would you like to move" is not "are you applying." Actual visa demand is measured by petition volumes, not poll intent.

If you're planning a move, treat this poll as **context, not a forecast**. It tells you how crowded the *aspirational* field is, not how hard your specific visa will be to get.

## Step-by-Step: Turning Migration Intent Into an Actual U.S. Plan

Whether the global number is up or down, the mechanics of moving are the same. Here's the practical sequence:

1. **Classify your pathway.** Employment-based (H-1B, EB-2/EB-3), family-based, investment (EB-5), or student-to-work (F-1 → OPT → H-1B). Each has different queues and timelines.
2. **Check current priority dates.** For employment-based categories, the Visa Bulletin's cutoff dates determine when you can file. A lower global intent number does not move these dates—Congressional caps do.
3. **Audit your country of chargeability.** The per-country 7% cap means applicants from India and China face far longer waits than the global average. This is the single biggest variable in your timeline.
4. **Build a credential file early.** Degree evaluations, employer sponsorship letters, and police clearances take months. Start before you need them.
5. **Budget realistically.** Filing fees, attorney costs, and relocation typically run $5,000–$15,000 for employment-based cases, more for EB-5.
6. **Track petition volumes, not polls.** USCIS quarterly H-1B registration data and the Visa Bulletin are your real leading indicators.

## Common Misreadings and What You Should Know

- **"New low means the U.S. is less popular."** Not necessarily—it means fewer people *say* they want to move. Policy, not popularity, governs admissions.
- **"Fewer applicants means faster processing."** False. Backlogs are driven by statutory caps and agency capacity, not global sentiment.
- **"The poll predicts visa reform."** It doesn't. Polling has no direct legislative effect.
- **Watch the regional data, not the global number.** If intent from your region is stable or rising, assume competition for your category is unchanged.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What does the Gallup migration poll actually ask?
It asks adults in over 100 countries whether they would like to move permanently to another country, and if so, which one. The U.S. figure is the share naming the U.S. as their preferred destination.

### Does a lower desire to migrate to the U.S. make getting a visa easier?
No. Visa availability is set by statutory caps and the Visa Bulletin, not by global polling sentiment. Your country of chargeability and category matter far more.

### Which countries still show the highest intent to move to the U.S.?
Historically, Latin America and Sub-Saharan Africa show the highest intent, well above the global average. Check the regional breakdown in the full Gallup release for the 2026 figures.

## Final Verdict

Don't let a headline number change your plans. If you're serious about a U.S. move, **ignore the global average and focus on your category, your country of chargeability, and the Visa Bulletin.** The Gallup poll is useful context for understanding global sentiment—it is not a predictor of your visa timeline. Build your credential file now, track petition volumes instead of polls, and budget for the real costs. The people who succeed at this are the ones who plan around the mechanics, not the mood.
