## Meta Employees Denounce Interference: How to Verify the Claims Yourself

When Meta employees publicly denounce interference in Brazilian elections, the headlines fly fast — but the practical question most readers actually have is simpler: **how do I check whether what I'm seeing on Facebook, Instagram, or WhatsApp is real?** This guide skips the punditry and gives you a concrete, repeatable workflow to audit political content, verify whistleblower claims, and fact-check election material using free, publicly available tools. Whether you're a journalist, researcher, or just a concerned voter, the steps below let you move from "I heard something" to "here's the evidence."

## Step-by-Step Guide: Auditing Political Content on Meta Platforms

The allegation centers on interference in Brazilian elections. To evaluate any such claim, you need to pull primary evidence — not screenshots of screenshots. Here's the workflow:

### Step 1: Use the Meta Ad Library (Free, No Login Required for Political Ads)
1. Go to `facebook.com/ads/library`.
2. Select country: **Brazil**.
3. Choose category: **Issues, Elections or Politics**.
4. Filter by date range around the election period you're investigating.
5. Search advertiser names, candidate names, or keywords in Portuguese (e.g., "eleição," "voto," "candidato").

The Ad Library retains political ads for **seven years** and shows spend ranges, impressions, and targeting demographics. If a whistleblower claims ads were suppressed or boosted, the spend and impression data is your first cross-check.

### Step 2: Cross-Reference With the WhatsApp Business API Transparency
Brazil's election integrity debate is heavily tied to WhatsApp. Meta publishes a **WhatsApp Threat Report** quarterly. Compare reported takedowns against the timeframe in any whistleblower account. Discrepancies between reported enforcement and alleged interference are the crux of most disputes.

### Step 3: Verify the Source Article and Discussion
- Read the original report at the article URL (panamericandispatch.org) and note **who** is making the claim, **what specific mechanism** they allege (algorithmic amplification, content moderation suppression, ad review bias), and **what evidence** they cite.
- Check the Hacker News thread (`news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50248061`) for technical pushback — commenters often flag missing data or logical gaps within hours.

### Step 4: Run Independent Fact-Checks
Use these free tools to validate specific viral claims:
- **Google Fact Check Explorer** — search "Brazil election Meta."
- **AFP Checamos** and **Aos Fatos** — Brazil's leading Portuguese-language fact-checkers.
- **Snopes / PolitiFact** — for English-language claims that crossed over.

### Step 5: Document Everything
Screenshot with timestamps, save Ad Library permalinks, and archive pages via the **Wayback Machine**. Claims about deleted content are unverifiable without archives.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### How do I check if a political ad on Facebook was paid for by a foreign actor?
Open the Ad Library, click the ad, and review the **"Confirmed organization"** and **"Paid for by"** fields. Meta requires identity verification for political advertisers in Brazil; unverified spend is a red flag worth reporting.

### Is the Meta Ad Library available for Brazilian election ads from past years?
Yes. Political and issue ads are retained for **seven years**, so 2022 and 2024 Brazilian election ads remain searchable by date, advertiser, and keyword.

### Can I verify whistleblower claims about Meta algorithm changes?
Only partially. Meta does not publish ranking algorithm details, so independent verification relies on **academic audit studies** (e.g., algorithmic auditing papers) and leaked documents. Cross-reference multiple sources before accepting any single claim.
