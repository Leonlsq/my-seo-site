## Carta + NewView Tender Offers: How Startup Employees Can Actually Cash Out Early

If you're holding vested but illiquid startup equity and wondering whether the new Carta–NewView partnership changes anything for you, here's the short answer: **it makes the paperwork and transaction mechanics of a tender offer dramatically easier, but it does not automatically make your shares sellable.** The program standardizes how companies run tender offers (the structured buyback events where employees sell vested shares to investors), reducing the legal and administrative friction that historically made founders and boards avoid them. Your ability to sell still depends on your employer opting in, your vesting status, and your company's 409A valuation. This guide breaks down exactly how the process works, what to check before you count on liquidity, and the tax traps that catch first-time sellers.

## How the Carta–NewView Tender Offer Program Works (Step-by-Step)

The core idea: NewView provides the capital and deal infrastructure to buy employee shares, while Carta provides the cap-table, equity, and transaction rails that most startups already use. Together they aim to turn a months-long legal slog into a repeatable, lower-cost event.

Here's the practical flow if your company participates:

1. **Company opts in.** Your employer (with board approval) schedules a tender offer window. This is the gating step — nothing happens without it.
2. **Eligibility check.** Typically limited to employees with **vested** shares who meet a minimum tenure (often 1–2 years). Contractors and advisors are frequently excluded.
3. **409A valuation review.** The offer price is anchored to your company's most recent independent 409A valuation. Expect a discount to the "preferred" price investors paid — often **10–30% below** the last preferred round.
4. **Election window.** You choose how many vested shares to sell (partial sales are usually allowed) during a set window, commonly 2–4 weeks.
5. **Documentation via Carta.** Signature packets, ROFR (right of first refusal) waivers, and transfer paperwork are executed digitally instead of through DocuSign chains and email threads.
6. **Closing and payment.** Funds are wired after the window closes and the company waives its ROFR. Timeline is typically **30–90 days** from election to cash.

### Key Parameters to Verify Before You Sell

| Item | What to Check | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Vesting status | Vested vs. unvested shares in Carta | Only vested shares are sellable |
| 409A price | Latest valuation date & price | Sets your offer baseline |
| Preferred price | Last priced round | Shows your discount |
| ROFR | Company/investor rights | Can delay or block the sale |
| Tax basis | Grant price / FMV at exercise | Determines capital gains vs. ordinary income |
| Lockup/blackout | Any trading restrictions | May bar you from participating |

## Common Issues, Tax Traps, and What You Should Know

**The tax reality is the biggest gotcha.** For ISO (incentive stock option) holders, a tender offer sale is a *disqualifying disposition* if you sell within 2 years of grant or 1 year of exercise — meaning your gains get taxed as **ordinary income**, not long-term capital gains. Run the numbers before electing; sometimes waiting a few months saves thousands.

**Discount expectations.** Early-stage tender offers rarely pay the headline valuation. A company last valued at $500M might tender at a per-share price implying $350–400M. That's normal — buyers demand a liquidity discount for illiquid, minority stakes.

**It's not a guarantee of future liquidity.** Participating in one tender offer doesn't obligate your company to run another. Don't build a financial plan around recurring cash-outs.

**Watch for concentration risk.** Selling only a fraction (many programs cap sales at **10–25% of vested holdings**) is common. If you can only sell a slice, prioritize covering taxes and diversifying rather than timing the market.

**Alternatives if your company hasn't opted in:** secondary marketplaces (Forge, EquityZen) — though these often require company consent and have their own fees — or simply waiting for a future event. The Carta–NewView program's real value is lowering the *company's* cost and hassle of saying yes, which should gradually expand access.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Can any startup employee sell shares through the Carta–NewView program?
No. Your employer must opt into the program and run a tender offer with board approval. Carta and NewView provide the infrastructure; they can't force a company to offer liquidity.

### How much does it cost employees to sell shares in a tender offer?
Employees typically pay **no direct transaction fee** — costs are usually borne by the company or baked into the buyer's discount. However, you may owe significant income or capital gains tax depending on your option type and holding period.

### Is selling in a tender offer taxable?
Yes. Sales of vested shares are taxable events. ISO holders selling before the qualifying holding period face ordinary income tax on the spread; NSO and RSU holders have their own treatment. Consult a tax advisor before electing.

### How long does it take to get paid?
Usually **30–90 days** after the election window closes, once the company waives its right of first refusal and closing documents are executed through Carta.

## Final Verdict

The Carta–NewView partnership is genuinely useful — but treat it as **infrastructure, not a promise**. If your company announces a tender offer, the smart move is to verify your vesting and 409A price in Carta, model the after-tax proceeds (especially if you hold ISOs), and sell a diversified slice rather than betting everything on one liquidity event. If your startup hasn't opted in, ask your CFO or head of people whether the program is on the roadmap — lower admin friction is exactly the argument that gets boards to say yes.
