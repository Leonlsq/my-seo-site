## The Heat Ray Is Back: What the Pentagon-DHS Deal Actually Means

If you just saw the headline that the Pentagon granted the Department of Homeland Security permission to test a "heat ray weapon" at the border, here is the short version: this is the **Active Denial System (ADS)**, a millimeter-wave directed-energy device that heats the top layer of skin to create a burning sensation without causing physical injury. The October 2026 agreement lets DHS evaluate the system in a border environment — but it is a **testing authorization, not deployment**. Below is a practical breakdown of what the device does, what it can't do, and how you can track or comment on the program.

## What the Active Denial System Actually Does

The ADS is a vehicle-mounted or fixed platform that fires a beam in the **95 GHz** band. Energy penetrates roughly **0.4 mm (about 1/64 inch)** into the skin — shallow enough to trigger pain receptors without damaging deeper tissue. The Pentagon has studied it for decades, and it has never been used in a domestic operational role.

| Parameter | Detail |
|---|---|
| Technology | Millimeter-wave directed energy (95 GHz) |
| Penetration depth | ~0.4 mm into skin |
| Effective range | Roughly 500–1,000 m in lab/field tests |
| Beam width | Approximately 2 m at 100 m distance |
| Reported effect | Intense heating sensation within seconds |
| Injury risk | Low at tested exposure levels; burns possible at close range or prolonged exposure |
| Status | Testing authorization only — not an approved operational weapon |

### How the testing phase likely works

1. **Site selection** — DHS would identify a controlled border segment with low civilian density.
2. **Safety protocols** — Exposure limits, medical monitoring, and shutoff procedures would be defined before any trial.
3. **Data collection** — Effectiveness at deterring approach, environmental interference (dust, rain, heat), and operator usability.
4. **Evaluation report** — Findings would feed into a decision on whether to pursue operational deployment, which would likely require further legal and policy review.
5. **Public and congressional scrutiny** — Expect oversight hearings and FOIA requests; the program is politically sensitive.

## Common Concerns and What You Should Know

- **It is not a "ray gun."** The ADS causes pain, not incapacitation. People can and do walk out of the beam.
- **Weather matters.** Heavy rain, dust, and humidity can degrade beam performance — a key reason border testing is being proposed.
- **Legal questions are unresolved.** Domestic use of a pain-inducing device raises Fourth Amendment and use-of-force policy questions that testing alone does not settle.
- **It has a long, stalled history.** The military shelved earlier ADS deployments over practicality and public-relations concerns; this authorization revives a program that has repeatedly failed to reach operational status.
- **Alternatives exist.** Long-range acoustic devices, surveillance towers, and physical barriers are already deployed and have their own documented controversies.

### How to track the program and submit comments

- Monitor **DHS and Pentagon press releases** and the **Federal Register** for any formal rulemaking or environmental assessment tied to the testing.
- File **FOIA requests** with DHS (dhs.gov/foia) for testing protocols and safety documentation.
- Contact your **congressional representative** — directed-energy programs fall under defense and homeland security oversight committees.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Is the heat ray weapon legal to use on US soil?
Not yet established. The current authorization covers **testing only**. Operational domestic use would require additional legal review, and civil-liberties groups are likely to challenge it.

### Can the Active Denial System cause burns?
At tested exposure levels, the Pentagon reports the effect is temporary and non-injurious. However, close-range or prolonged exposure, or use on people with certain medical conditions, could raise injury risk — which is why testing protocols and medical oversight matter.

### Is the heat ray at the border actually deployed right now?
No. As of the October 2026 reporting, DHS has **permission to test**, not to deploy. Any operational rollout would be a separate, publicly visible decision.

## Final Verdict

Treat this as the start of a **testing and oversight cycle**, not a finished deployment. If you care about this issue, the highest-leverage actions are tracking Federal Register notices, filing FOIA requests, and pressuring congressional oversight committees — because the real decision point comes after the test data lands.
