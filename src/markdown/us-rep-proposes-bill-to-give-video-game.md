## Game Ownership Bill Explained: What Changes for Your Digital Library

A US Representative has introduced a bill that would require video game sellers to grant buyers **ownership** of a game rather than a revocable **license**—a direct challenge to the EULA model that has governed digital storefronts for two decades. Here's the practical takeaway: **nothing changes in your Steam, PlayStation, or Xbox library today.** The bill faces significant technical and legislative hurdles, and even if passed, enforcement mechanics (DRM, account binding, server dependencies) remain unresolved. This guide breaks down what the bill actually proposes, how it would affect your existing purchases, and the concrete steps you can take right now to protect your library regardless of the outcome.

## What the Bill Actually Proposes (And What It Doesn't)

The core legal shift is from a *license* (permission to access content, revocable under terms of service) to *ownership* (a property interest the seller cannot unilaterally terminate). The distinction matters most in three scenarios:

- **Store shutdowns**: When a storefront closes (e.g., Nintendo Wii U eShop, 3DS eShop), licensed content becomes inaccessible. Ownership would theoretically require a preservation or transfer mechanism.
- **Account bans**: Under a license, a ToS violation can revoke access to your entire library. Ownership would limit revocation to the specific title or require refund.
- **Resale and transfer**: Ownership implies a right of first sale—the legal basis for reselling physical discs. Digital resale would require a transfer mechanism that most platforms currently lack.

What the bill does **not** do (as drafted in typical proposals of this type):

| Claim | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Games become DRM-free" | No. Ownership doesn't remove copy protection; it changes the legal status of your access. |
| "You can resell Steam games tomorrow" | No. No transfer infrastructure exists; resale requires platform-level implementation. |
| "Online games stay playable forever" | No. Server-dependent games (live service titles) can't be preserved by ownership alone. |
| "Retroactive to all past purchases" | Unclear. Most proposals apply prospectively or require seller compliance timelines. |

## How to Protect Your Library Right Now (Step-by-Step)

Regardless of the bill's fate, these steps reduce your exposure to license revocation:

1. **Audit your library for server dependencies.** Sort your Steam/console library by "last played" and flag titles with online-only components (e.g., *Anthem*, *LawBreakers*). These are the highest-risk titles under any licensing model.
2. **Prioritize DRM-free purchases where possible.** GOG sells DRM-free installers you can back up locally. For Steam titles, check [SteamDB](https://steamdb.info) or the PCGamingWiki for DRM status before buying.
3. **Back up installers and offline modes.** For single-player games, verify offline play works: disconnect your network, launch the game, and confirm it runs. If it doesn't, document the required online check-in frequency.
4. **Export your purchase records.** Take screenshots or export receipts from Steam (Account Details → Purchase History), PlayStation, and Xbox. These are your proof of purchase if ownership claims ever need to be asserted.
5. **Avoid pre-ordering delisted-risk titles.** Games with licensed IP (sports, movie tie-ins, racing games with real car brands) are delisted most often. Buy them early and back them up.
6. **Use family sharing and account recovery tools.** Enable two-factor authentication and store recovery codes—account loss is the most common way players lose library access today.

## The Technical Hurdles Nobody Talks About

Even a passed bill faces implementation problems that make "ownership" legally meaningful but practically hollow:

- **DRM servers**: Many games phone home to authentication servers. Ownership doesn't compel publishers to keep those servers running.
- **Account binding**: Your library is tied to a single account. True ownership would require a transfer or inheritance mechanism—none exists at scale.
- **Live service economics**: Games like *Destiny 2* or *Suicide Squad* depend on ongoing server costs. Ownership can't force a publisher to fund servers indefinitely.
- **Platform fragmentation**: Steam, Epic, PlayStation, and Xbox each have separate licensing architectures. A US bill doesn't automatically apply to international storefronts or console manufacturers' global terms.

**Comparison: License vs. Ownership in practice**

| Scenario | Current License Model | Proposed Ownership Model |
|---|---|---|
| Store delisting | Game may become unpurchasable but stays in library | Same, unless preservation mandate added |
| Account ban | Full library access revoked | Title-specific revocation or refund |
| Resale | Prohibited by EULA | Requires transfer infrastructure (not built) |
| Inheritance | Account sharing violates ToS | Would require legal transfer path |
| Offline play | Depends on DRM | Unchanged |

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Will this bill let me resell my Steam games?
Not automatically. Ownership establishes a legal right of first sale, but digital resale requires a transfer mechanism (like a marketplace or key revocation system) that Valve has not built. Expect years of implementation debate before digital resale becomes practical.

### Does the game ownership bill apply to games I already own?
Most proposals apply prospectively or include compliance windows for existing purchases. Retroactive application is legally complex and would likely face court challenges. Your current library remains governed by existing EULAs until enforcement rules are finalized.

### What happens to my games if Steam shuts down?
Under current licenses, you lose access to anything requiring Steam authentication. Ownership laws could mandate offline activation or installer distribution, but no such mechanism exists today. Back up DRM-free titles and verify offline modes now.

## Final Verdict

**Don't wait for legislation to protect your library.** The ownership bill is a meaningful signal that license terms are under scrutiny, but technical and legislative hurdles mean practical change is years away—if it arrives at all. Your best move today: audit your library for server-dependent titles, back up DRM-free installers, export purchase records, and favor stores like GOG when ownership matters to you. Treat digital purchases as long-term rentals until the law—and the infrastructure—catches up.
