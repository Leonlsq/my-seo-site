## How to Verify the Viral Jensen Huang 'Affair Letter' Before You Share It

If you landed here after seeing a screenshot of an alleged letter from Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang to staff about an affair, you are likely searching for the original source or wondering whether it is real. Here is the direct takeaway: **treat any unverified letter screenshot as fabricated until you can match it to a primary source.** This guide walks you through the exact verification steps to confirm whether a document, screenshot, or "leaked" memo is authentic — and how to avoid amplifying AI-generated fakes that spread alongside real Nvidia news cycles.

This matters because Nvidia sits at the center of a genuine, high-profile news cycle involving federal investigations and espionage reporting. When real news is swirling, fabricated documents attach themselves to the noise and spread faster than corrections. The verification workflow below is your defense.

## Step-by-Step: Verify a Viral Letter or Screenshot in Under 5 Minutes

Use this checklist every time a "leaked memo" crosses your feed.

1. **Trace the image to its first appearance.** Run the screenshot through Google Lens, Yandex Images, or TinEye. Sort results by "oldest." If the earliest upload is a random social post with no news outlet attached, that is a red flag.
2. **Check for the primary document.** Legitimate corporate letters surface through official channels: Nvidia's newsroom, SEC filings (8-K, DEF 14A), or a verified reporter's byline at a major outlet. If no outlet has the actual document, assume it does not exist.
3. **Inspect the metadata.** Download the image and check EXIF data (right-click → Properties on Windows, or Get Info on Mac). AI-generated or re-saved screenshots often strip metadata entirely or show editing software in the "Software" field.
4. **Look for AI tells in the text.** Zoom to 200% and scan for:
   - Inconsistent font kerning or letter spacing
   - Smudged or doubled letterforms around punctuation
   - Paragraph alignment that drifts
   - Overly generic corporate phrasing with no specific names, dates, or figures
5. **Cross-reference the timeline.** Real memos have a date, distribution list, and follow-up reporting within 24–48 hours. A letter with no corroborating second source after several days is almost certainly fake.
6. **Check the outlet, not the screenshot.** Go directly to the publisher's site (Axios, NYT, CNN) and search their own site. If the story is not there, the "screenshot of the article" is fabricated.

## Common Issues, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

**The "screenshot of a headline" trap.** Most viral fakes are not the letter itself — they are doctored screenshots of real headlines from Axios, the New York Times, or CNN. Always open the URL in a browser rather than trusting the image. If the article loads and the headline differs, you have found a manipulated screenshot.

**Archive links are your friend.** When a real article exists, save it to archive.ph or the Wayback Machine immediately. Fakes rarely survive archiving because the archived page will not match the doctored screenshot. If someone sends you an archive link, verify the archive timestamp matches the claimed publication date.

**AI text detectors are unreliable.** Tools like GPTZero or Originality.ai give false positives on short corporate text. Do not rely on them as your sole check — use the metadata and corroboration steps above instead.

**Do not engage with the rumor directly.** Sharing a fake "to debunk it" still spreads the image and its metadata. Screenshot the debunk, not the fake. Report the original post to the platform.

**For Nvidia investors and employees:** the only authoritative sources are Nvidia's investor relations page, official SEC filings, and direct company communications. Any letter circulating outside those channels should be treated as unverified until a named reporter confirms it with a byline.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Is the Jensen Huang affair letter real?
No credible primary source has published the letter itself. Until a named reporter at a major outlet confirms the document with a byline and the company responds, treat any circulating version as unverified or fabricated.

### How can I tell if a news screenshot is AI-generated?
Check for font inconsistencies, stripped EXIF metadata, and whether the headline matches the live article at the source URL. If the live page shows a different headline, the screenshot is manipulated.

### Where can I find the original Nvidia news coverage?
Go directly to the publisher's website — Axios, the New York Times, or CNN — and search their site. Use archive.ph links only to confirm a specific article's content and timestamp, not as proof of a viral screenshot.

### What should I do if I already shared a fake letter?
Delete the post, then post a correction linking to a verified source. Deleting removes the image and its metadata from your feed and reduces further spread.
