## Artemis II vs New Glenn: What Actually Happened and How to Track It

If you searched for "Rocket Report" this week and got whiplash, you're not alone. Two headlines collided: **Artemis II literally tore through the atmosphere** on its crewed lunar flyby, while **New Glenn hit another delay** — and buried in the same news cycle, **Interstellar Technologies announced fresh government funding for its Japanese Zero rocket**. The direct takeaway: this is a *tracking and verification* problem, not a news problem. Mainstream outlets gave you the headline; this guide gives you the tools, timelines, and comparison data to follow all three programs yourself without refreshing five tabs.

Here's the practical breakdown of what each event means, how to monitor the missions in real time, and what the delays and funding actually signal for the commercial launch market.

## The Three Programs Side-by-Side: Specs and Status

Before you chase livestreams, understand what you're watching. These are three very different vehicles at very different maturity stages.

| Program | Operator | Class | Status (Nov 2026) | Key Metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Artemis II | NASA / SLS | Super-heavy crewed | Completed lunar flyby | ~8.8M lbf thrust (SLS Block 1) |
| New Glenn | Blue Origin | Heavy-lift reusable | Delayed again | ~3.9M lbf thrust (7× BE-4) |
| Zero | Interstellar Technologies | Small-lift | New gov. funding round | ~100 kg to LEO class |

### Why "tore through the atmosphere" matters
Artemis II's ascent is the highest-energy crewed launch profile since Apollo. The phrase isn't hype — the SLS core stage plus twin five-segment boosters produce roughly **15% more thrust than Saturn V** at liftoff. For tracking purposes, that means a compressed ascent timeline: key events (booster separation, core stage cutoff, ICPS burn) happen faster than most casual viewers expect, so set alerts early.

### Why New Glenn keeps slipping
Blue Origin's New Glenn delay is the recurring story of the program. Each slip typically traces to one of three buckets: **BE-4 engine qualification**, **stage recovery/landing hardware**, or **range and regulatory scheduling**. When you read the next delay headline, check which bucket it falls into — that tells you whether it's a weeks-long or quarters-long problem.

## How to Track All Three Launches in Real Time

Follow this sequence to avoid missing events and to verify claims yourself.

1. **Bookmark primary sources first.** NASA's blog for Artemis, Blue Origin's mission page for New Glenn, and Interstellar Technologies' press page for Zero. Skip aggregators until you've confirmed the primary timestamp.
2. **Set T-0 alerts, not "launch day" alerts.** Use the operator's own countdown or a tracker like Next Spaceflight. Delays are announced at T-minus hours, not days.
3. **Watch the ascent event ladder.** For Artemis II: booster sep → core cutoff → ICPS ignition → TLI burn. For New Glenn: Max-Q → main engine cutoff → stage sep → booster landing attempt.
4. **Cross-check the "delay" reason.** Confirm whether the scrub was **weather**, **technical**, or **range conflict** — each has a different re-attempt window.
5. **For Zero, track funding, not launches.** Interstellar Technologies' government funding news is a *capital* signal, not a flight event. Follow their investor/press releases for milestone dates.

## What the Delays and Funding Actually Signal

This is where you get information the headlines skip.

- **New Glenn delays compress the commercial market.** Every slip hands more near-term heavy-lift manifest to competitors and pushes Blue Origin's reusable-cost story further out. If you're modeling launch costs, don't price New Glenn savings into 2026–2027 budgets yet.
- **Artemis II success de-risks the crewed deep-space cadence.** A clean flyby validates life-support and reentry at lunar-return velocities — the hardest part of any crewed architecture.
- **Japan's Zero funding is the quiet story.** Government backing for Interstellar Technologies signals Japan wants sovereign small-lift capacity. That's a long-game play for dedicated smallsat launches, not a direct New Glenn competitor.

### Common pitfalls when following this news
- **Confusing a scrub with a failure.** A weather scrub is routine; a hardware abort is not. Read the wording.
- **Trusting a single timestamp.** Time zones and "NET" (No Earlier Than) dates trip up most readers.
- **Overweighting funding announcements.** Money announced ≠ money deployed ≠ rocket flying.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Did Artemis II really launch and complete its lunar flyby?
Yes — per the trending report, Artemis II executed its crewed lunar flyby, with the SLS tearing through the atmosphere on ascent. Always verify the final mission status on NASA's official blog, as post-launch updates can revise timeline details.

### Why was New Glenn delayed again?
New Glenn delays typically stem from BE-4 engine qualification, recovery hardware, or range scheduling. The specific cause varies per slip — check Blue Origin's mission page for the stated reason before assuming it's an engine issue.

### What is Japan's Zero rocket and who builds it?
The Zero is a small-lift rocket developed by Interstellar Technologies. The recent news is a new round of **government funding**, which supports development and launch cadence rather than announcing an imminent flight.

### Is New Glenn worth waiting for compared to other heavy-lift options?
For reusable heavy-lift economics, yes in the long run — but not on a 2026 timeline. Until it flies consistently, treat its cost advantages as unproven and plan around established providers.
