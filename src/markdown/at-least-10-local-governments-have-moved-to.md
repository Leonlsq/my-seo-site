## How to Get Flock Surveillance Cameras Removed From Your City

Since summer 2026, at least 10 local governments across the U.S. have moved to cancel or let lapse their contracts with Flock Safety, the license plate reader (ALPR) company operating tens of thousands of cameras nationwide. The stated reasons are consistent: privacy overreach, unclear data-sharing with federal agencies and out-of-state police, and procurement processes that skipped public oversight. If you've heard about this in your own town and want to know what you can actually *do*—or you're a council member, journalist, or privacy advocate who needs a concrete playbook—this guide walks through the exact steps to audit, challenge, and potentially terminate a Flock deployment.

The short version: you don't need to be a lawyer or a councilmember to start. You need public records, a copy of the contract, and a list of the specific questions that have already forced other cities to cancel.

## Step-by-Step: How to Challenge a Flock Contract in Your City

### Step 1: File a Public Records Request for the Contract
Every Flock deployment is governed by a contract with the municipality or its police department. Submit a written public records request (often called a FOIA or state-equivalent request) to your city clerk and police department asking for:

- The **master services agreement** and any addenda with Flock Safety
- **Data-sharing settings** enabled in the Flock Portal (e.g., "National Lookup," state fusion center access, ICE/CBP sharing)
- **Procurement records**: was this a sole-source purchase, a cooperative contract, or a competitive bid?
- **Cost**: per-camera pricing, subscription term, and total contract value

Most records requests are answered within 5–20 business days depending on state law. This is the single highest-leverage step—the contract usually reveals whether data sharing was ever disclosed to the public.

### Step 2: Audit the Data-Sharing Configuration
Flock's default settings have historically allowed agencies to opt into broad sharing. Ask specifically whether your department enabled:

| Setting | What It Does | Privacy Risk |
|---|---|---|
| National Lookup | Lets any Flock agency nationwide query your plates | High |
| State/Fusion Center Sharing | Feeds state intelligence hubs | Medium–High |
| Federal Agency Access | Shares with ICE, CBP, FBI | High |
| Out-of-State Sharing | Cross-jurisdiction queries | Medium |

If your department can't or won't confirm these settings in writing, that's a legitimate council question—and it's the exact issue that drove several 2026 cancellations.

### Step 3: Bring It to a Public Meeting
Request a spot on the agenda for public comment, and submit written questions to your council member in advance. The most effective framing isn't "surveillance is bad"—it's procedural: *Was this procurement competitively bid? Were data-sharing terms disclosed? What's the retention period, and who audits access?*

### Step 4: Request the Audit Logs
Ask how many queries your department ran, who ran them, and whether any were run for non-criminal purposes (e.g., immigration enforcement or personal reasons). Audit-log transparency is a common contract-renewal condition.

### Step 5: Push for a Renewal Vote
Most Flock contracts auto-renew annually. Identify the renewal date and lobby for an explicit council vote rather than automatic renewal—this is how several cities effectively ended deployments without litigation.

## Common Issues, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

- **"Sole-source" procurement is the weak point.** Many deployments skipped competitive bidding. If yours did, that's often the cleanest legal challenge.
- **Cancellation isn't always clean.** Some contracts have early-termination fees or multi-year commitments. Check the termination clause before promising your council a free exit.
- **Alternatives exist.** Fixed ALPR is not the only option—some cities use mobile readers, targeted camera placements, or none at all. Ask what problem the cameras solve and whether a narrower tool does it.
- **Data already shared may persist.** Even after cancellation, check whether historical data was retained by third parties or federal agencies.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Can I get my city's Flock cameras removed as a private citizen?
Not directly—but you can file records requests, speak at council meetings, and pressure elected officials. Removal happens through contract non-renewal or council vote, which is exactly how the 10+ cities since summer 2026 did it.

### Are Flock cameras illegal?
No. They're legal in most jurisdictions, but legality isn't the issue—the concerns are data-sharing scope, procurement transparency, and retention. Several cities dropped them on policy and procurement grounds, not criminal ones.

### How much do Flock contracts cost?
Pricing varies by camera count and term, typically in the range of a few thousand dollars per camera per year. Request the exact figure via public records—it's often lower than expected, which weakens the "we can't afford to lose it" argument.

## Final Verdict

If you want your city to drop Flock, skip the outrage and start with paperwork: **file the records request, get the contract, and force a public vote at renewal.** The data-sharing settings and procurement records are where these deployments are most vulnerable—and they're the same levers that worked in the cities that have already walked away. Do that, and you're not protesting a camera; you're holding a contract accountable.
