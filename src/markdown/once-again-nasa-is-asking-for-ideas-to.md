## NASA Wants to Save Hubble Again — Here's How a Real Rescue Would Work

For the second time in four years, NASA has issued a formal request for ideas to reboost the Hubble Space Telescope — and this time the stakes are higher. Hubble's orbit is decaying at roughly **0.1 km per year**, and without intervention, the 34-year-old observatory is projected to re-enter Earth's atmosphere sometime in the **mid-to-late 2030s**. In 2022, NASA ran a similar Request for Information (RFI) through its **Voyager 2-style commercial partnership model**, but no funded mission emerged. This guide breaks down exactly how a Hubble reboost would work, what went wrong last time, and what you should watch for as new proposals come in.

## How a Hubble Reboost Mission Would Actually Work

Reboosting Hubble is not like refueling a satellite. Hubble was designed for **servicing by the Space Shuttle**, which retired in 2011. Any new mission must dock with or grapple Hubble without astronaut EVA support (or with a commercial crew vehicle), then raise its orbit by roughly **50–100 km** to extend its life by 15–20 years.

Here's the step-by-step mission profile NASA is evaluating:

1. **Rendezvous and proximity operations** — A commercial spacecraft (SpaceX Dragon, a Northrop Grumman vehicle, or a purpose-built tug) must match Hubble's orbit at ~535 km altitude and close to within meters.
2. **Capture or docking** — Hubble has no standardized docking port. Options include a robotic arm grapple on the aft shroud, a custom docking adapter, or a "soft capture" ring installed during a prior mission.
3. **Orbit raise burn** — A controlled thruster burn lifts the telescope to a stable orbit. Required delta-v is small (tens of m/s), but precision matters to avoid stressing the 12-ton observatory.
4. **Release and verification** — The tug separates, and ground teams at STScI verify pointing stability and instrument health.
5. **Optional servicing** — Some proposals bundle gyroscope replacement or new instruments, but NASA's current RFI focuses on **reboost only** for cost reasons.

## Why the 2022 Hubble Reboost Plan Failed — and What's Different Now

The 2022 RFI attracted interest from SpaceX (using a Crew Dragon) and others, but the effort stalled for three concrete reasons:

- **No budget line.** NASA's astrophysics division never allocated funding; the RFI was exploratory only.
- **Risk vs. reward.** Hubble's science output is still strong, but the agency weighed reboost costs against the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope (launching ~2027) and James Webb operations.
- **Technical uncertainty.** No commercial vehicle had demonstrated uncrewed capture of a non-cooperative satellite at Hubble's altitude and inclination.

What's changed by 2026: **commercial rendezvous and proximity operations have matured**, several companies now offer satellite life-extension tugs, and Hubble's gyroscope situation has worsened — three of six gyros have failed, pushing the telescope into single-gyro mode. That makes the case for a combined reboost-and-servicing mission stronger than in 2022.

## Common Issues, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

If you're following this story, here's the practical context most news coverage skips:

- **This is not a "Hubble is dying tomorrow" story.** The telescope remains scientifically productive through at least the early 2030s even without a reboost.
- **Alternatives to reboost:** NASA could let Hubble deorbit naturally and rely on Webb plus Roman. A controlled deorbit (using a tug to steer it into the ocean) is also being studied for safety reasons.
- **Cost-benefit reality:** A $500M reboost buys roughly 15 years of Hubble time — competitive with a small dedicated mission, which is why the idea keeps resurfacing.
- **Watch the RFI deadline.** Proposals are typically due within 60–90 days; the agency will then decide whether to fund a study or move to a formal solicitation.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Will NASA actually reboost Hubble in 2026?
No mission is funded yet. NASA has only issued a request for ideas; any actual reboost would launch no earlier than the late 2020s if a proposal is selected and funded.

### How long does Hubble have left without a reboost?
Current projections put atmospheric reentry in the **mid-to-late 2030s**, though the telescope could remain scientifically operational until the early 2030s before orbit decay becomes critical.

### Why can't SpaceX just grab Hubble with a Dragon?
Dragon lacks a docking interface compatible with Hubble and would require a custom capture mechanism. SpaceX proposed a Crew Dragon concept in 2022, but it was never funded or built.

### How much would a Hubble rescue mission cost?
Public estimates range from **$300 million to $1 billion**, depending on whether the mission is reboost-only or includes gyroscope and instrument servicing.

## Final Verdict

Don't expect a Hubble rescue launch anytime soon — this is an ideas solicitation, not a funded mission. But the 2026 RFI is more serious than 2022's: commercial servicing tech has matured, Hubble's gyros are failing, and the science case for 15 more years is strong. If you want to follow the story, watch for NASA's selection announcement in the next 6–12 months; that, not the RFI itself, is the real signal that Hubble's rescue is happening.
