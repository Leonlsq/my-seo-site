## Rewilding Islands to Beat Rising Seas: The Practical Playbook

If you've read the headlines about rewilding helping vulnerable islands "keep pace with rising seas," you're probably wondering what that actually means in practice — and whether it's a real engineering strategy or just hopeful ecology. The short answer: it's both, and it works through a specific mechanism called **biogenic accretion**. Coral reefs, seabird guano, and salt-tolerant native plants don't just sit on an island — they actively build it. This guide breaks down how the process works, what a realistic restoration project looks like, and where it fails.

The core insight is that atolls are not static sandbars. They are living structures. A healthy reef produces coral sand that washes onto the shore. Seabirds import marine nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus) from hundreds of miles away, fertilizing plants that trap that sand. Remove any one link — overfish the reef, kill the birds, clear the vegetation — and the island stops growing and starts eroding. Rewilding is the act of reconnecting those links.

## How Biogenic Island Building Actually Works: Step-by-Step

Here's the operational sequence used in restoration projects across the Pacific and Indian Oceans (e.g., Palmyra, Tetiaroa, and the Chagos Archipelago).

1. **Stabilize the reef first.** No reef = no sand supply. This means enforcing no-take marine protected areas (MPAs), banning dynamite fishing, and reducing runoff. Target: coral cover above 30% before expecting meaningful sediment production.
2. **Remove invasive predators.** Rats (*Rattus rattus*, *R. exulans*) are the single biggest blocker. A rat eradication on a 1–10 km² atoll typically costs **$150,000–$800,000** and uses aerial-broadcast brodifacoum bait. Success rates exceed 80% on islands with no human residents.
3. **Re-attract seabirds.** Once rats are gone, use **social attraction**: solar-powered speakers playing colony calls, decoy birds, and mirror boxes. Sooty terns and wedge-tailed shearwaters recolonize within 2–5 years. Each nesting pair deposits roughly **0.5–2 kg of guano per season**.
4. **Replant native vegetation.** Prioritize *Scaevola taccada*, *Tournefortia argentea*, and *Pemphis acidula*. These trap windblown sand and build dunes. Avoid coconut monocultures — they shade out ground-nesting birds.
5. **Monitor accretion.** Use RTK-GPS transects and drone photogrammetry every 6 months. A functioning system can add **1–10 mm of vertical elevation per year**, which is the range needed to match current sea-level rise (3–5 mm/yr) plus storm surge.

| Native replanting | $20k–$150k | 3–10 yrs | +1–5 mm/yr sand trapping |

## Common Pitfalls, Trade-Offs, and What You Should Know

Rewilding is not a silver bullet, and pretending otherwise gets projects defunded. Here's the honest ledger.

- **It only works with a living reef.** If ocean acidification has already killed coral cover below ~10%, you're looking at decades of recovery, not years. Prioritize emissions reduction alongside restoration.
- **Guano is a double-edged sword.** Too many birds on a small island can acidify soils and kill vegetation. Aim for balanced densities; monitor soil pH.
- **Human communities matter.** On inhabited atolls (e.g., in the Marshall Islands), rat eradication requires community consent, alternative protein sources, and biosecurity protocols to prevent reinvasion from boats.
- **Storm events can undo years of work.** A single Category 4 cyclone can strip 10 years of accretion. Design for resilience by restoring multiple islets, not just one.
- **Funding is fragmented.** Most projects rely on NGOs (Island Conservation, BirdLife), philanthropy, and sometimes blue-carbon credits. Budget 20% of total cost for long-term monitoring.

For comparison, hard engineering (seawalls, groynes) costs **$5,000–$20,000 per linear meter** and often accelerates erosion downdrift. Rewilding is slower but self-repairing and orders of magnitude cheaper per meter of coastline protected.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### How fast can rewilding help an island keep pace with sea-level rise?
A fully restored system — healthy reef, seabird colonies, native vegetation — can accrete **1–10 mm of vertical elevation per year**. Current global sea-level rise is roughly 3–5 mm/yr, so a functioning rewilded atoll can match or slightly exceed it, especially when storm-deposited sand is included.

### Can you rewild an island that already has rats and no seabirds?
Yes, but you must sequence it correctly: eradicate invasive predators first, then use social attraction (decoys, call playback) to lure birds, then replant natives. Skipping the eradication step wastes money because rats eat eggs, chicks, and seeds.

## Final Verdict

Rewilding is the most cost-effective, scalable tool we have for keeping low-lying atolls habitable as seas rise — but only if you treat it as a **system**, not a single fix. Start with the reef, remove the rats, bring back the birds, and let native plants do the engineering. If you're funding or planning a project, allocate at least 20% of your budget to monitoring and biosecurity, and expect visible accretion within 5–10 years, not 5–10 months. Skip the seawalls. Build the ecosystem instead.
