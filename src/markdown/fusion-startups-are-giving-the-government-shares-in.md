## Fusion Startups Weighing Government Equity: The Direct Answer

If you run a fusion startup and a government agency offers you a funding award in exchange for equity, the honest answer is: **it depends on your stage, your cap table, and which government you're talking to.** For most pre-Series B fusion companies burning $2M–$10M per year on plasma hardware, a non-dilutive-looking award with a modest equity kicker is usually worth it — *if* the equity stake stays under roughly 5% and doesn't come with board seats or IP encumbrances. It becomes a bad deal fast when the government wants double-digit ownership, liquidation preference, or control over licensing your reactor designs. This guide breaks down the actual math, the deal structures now circulating in the US, UK, and Japan, and a decision framework you can run in an afternoon.

## The Equity-for-Award Deal: What's Actually on the Table

Fusion is capital-hungry and pre-revenue. Private VC has cooled on 10-year timelines, so governments are stepping in with milestone-based awards — and increasingly attaching equity strings. Here's how the main structures compare.

| Deal Type | Typical Equity Taken | Cash Speed | Dilution Risk | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pure grant (no equity) | 0% | Slow (6–18 mo) | None | Early R&D, university spinouts |
| Milestone award + warrant | 1–5% (exercisable later) | Medium | Low–medium | Seed to Series A |
| Equity-for-award (direct) | 3–15% | Fast (3–6 mo) | Medium–high | Series A–B with hardware to build |
| Government co-investment | 5–20% + board seat | Fast | High | Late-stage, capital-intensive builds |

**Key parameters to negotiate before signing:**

1. **Ownership cap** — push for a hard ceiling (e.g., 5%) with anti-dilution protection waived.
2. **Warrant vs. shares** — warrants let you buy back the stake at a fixed price later; direct shares don't.
3. **IP carve-out** — confirm the government gets *no* rights to your reactor IP, diagnostics, or patents.
4. **Board and veto rights** — refuse board seats and any consent rights over hiring, M&A, or licensing.
5. **Milestone clawback terms** — know exactly what happens to the equity if you miss a physics milestone (you don't want to forfeit shares *and* return cash).

## Is It Worth It? Run the Dilution Math

The core question is simple: **does the award's cash value exceed the future value of the equity you give up?**

Worked example — a Series A fusion startup raising a $40M award for 6% equity:

- Post-money valuation at next round: assume $400M.
- Value of 6% at that round: **$24M**.
- Award cash received: **$40M**.
- Net benefit before dilution: **+$16M** — clearly worth it *if* you'd otherwise raise that $40M at a higher cost of capital or not at all.

Flip the assumptions — valuation hits $1B and you gave up 6%:

- Value of 6%: **$60M** vs. $40M cash → you effectively paid a **1.5x premium** for the money. Still often acceptable, because the alternative (no funding, delayed tokamak build) may be worth far more than the spread.

**Rule of thumb:** take the deal when the equity given up is worth less than 1.5x the cash at your *expected* next-round valuation, and walk when the government wants more than ~10% or any control rights.

## Go It Alone vs. Take the Deal: Decision Checklist

Use this to decide in one sitting:

- **Take the equity-for-award deal if:** you're pre-Series B, the stake is ≤5%, no board seat, IP is protected, and the cash unlocks a milestone that de-risks your next raise.
- **Walk away if:** the government demands >10%, liquidation preference, IP licensing rights, or milestone clawbacks that return cash *and* shares.
- **Hybrid path:** negotiate a warrant structure or a pure milestone grant with a small success fee instead of direct equity — this is the fastest-growing structure in 2026.
- **Shop jurisdictions:** US (DOE milestone programs), UK (UKAEA/UKIB-style co-investment), and Japan (NEDO) all have different equity appetites — the UK and Japan have been more flexible on caps than some US state-level programs.

## Common Pitfalls and What You Should Know

- **Hidden dilution:** equity awards often trigger anti-dilution clauses in existing VC term sheets — model the *combined* dilution, not just the government's slice.
- **Valuation anchoring:** a government share price can set a floor that spooks later private investors. Get a valuation cap written in.
- **Slow cash despite "fast" deals:** even equity deals can take 3–6 months to close; don't plan plasma procurement around the award date.
- **Political risk:** a change in government can freeze or renegotiate awards — diversify funding sources.
- **Alternatives:** strategic corporate investors (utilities, industrial gas firms) and sovereign wealth funds often offer better terms than equity-for-award programs.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Is giving the government equity for a fusion award worth it?
Usually yes for pre-Series B startups if the stake is 5% or less with no board seat or IP rights. It stops being worth it above ~10% ownership or when control rights are attached.

### How much equity do governments typically take in fusion funding deals?
Most current structures range from 1% to 15%, with warrants or milestone-linked shares being the most founder-friendly. Direct double-digit stakes usually signal a late-stage, capital-intensive build.

### Can I negotiate the equity terms of a government fusion award?
Yes. Ownership caps, warrant structures, IP carve-outs, and milestone clawback terms are all negotiable — bring a lawyer who has closed at least one government co-investment deal.

## Final Verdict

For most fusion startups, **the equity-for-award deal is worth taking — but only on your terms.** Cap the government's stake at 5%, insist on warrants or milestone-linked shares, protect your IP absolutely, and refuse board seats. If a program demands more than 10% plus control rights, go it alone or find a strategic private investor instead. Run the dilution math above before you sign anything; the difference between a good and a bad deal is usually a single negotiation clause, not the headline cash number.
