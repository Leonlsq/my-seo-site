## Flock Ignored the Cancellation—Here's How Cities Actually Force Camera Removal

When the Plymouth City Council voted to cancel its Flock Safety contract, the company didn't respond. So the police chief did what any fed-up customer does: he physically unplugged the cameras. This guide breaks down the exact playbook a city, council member, or resident can use to force removal of ALPR (automated license plate reader) cameras when a vendor stonewalls a cancellation—plus how to audit what data was already collected.

The short version: **a cancellation notice is not the same as a termination.** Contracts typically require written notice, a cure period, and a formal termination letter. If the vendor ignores you, you escalate to physical disconnection, data deletion demands, and public records leverage. Below is the step-by-step.

## Step-by-Step: How to Force Flock Camera Removal After Cancellation

### Step 1: Read the actual contract termination clause
Before anything else, pull the executed contract (it's usually a public record). Look for:
- **Notice period** — often 30–90 days written notice to a specific address/email.
- **Auto-renewal language** — many ALPR contracts auto-renew annually unless cancelled 60–90 days before the term ends.
- **Cure period** — time the vendor gets to fix a breach before termination is valid.
- **Data ownership clause** — who owns plate scans, and what deletion timeline applies after termination.

### Step 2: Send a formal, trackable termination notice
A council vote alone rarely triggers termination. Send a certified letter + email to the vendor's registered agent and contract contact. Include the contract number, effective termination date, and a demand for **written confirmation of data deletion** within a set window (e.g., 30 days).

### Step 3: Document non-response
Keep a paper trail: certified mail receipts, email read receipts, and timestamps. This is what justifies escalation—and what a city attorney needs if litigation becomes necessary.

### Step 4: Physically disconnect (the Plymouth move)
If the vendor won't confirm termination and the contract term has lapsed, disconnection is the enforcement step. Best practices:
- Photograph and log each camera's serial number and location before removal.
- Disconnect power and network, but **don't destroy hardware**—store it securely to avoid a property-damage counterclaim.
- Notify the vendor in writing that hardware is being held pending pickup instructions.

### Step 5: Demand data deletion in writing
Request a certificate of destruction for all plate scans, hotlist data, and audit logs. Under many state laws (and GDPR-style frameworks), you're entitled to confirmation that data was purged.

### Step 6: Escalate via public records and procurement
File a public records request for the vendor's data-sharing logs. Loop in your state's procurement office and, if applicable, the attorney general. Vendors respond faster to regulatory pressure than to a single city's emails.

## Common Issues, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

| Issue | What Happens | Workaround |
|---|---|---|
| Auto-renewal trap | Contract renews before you notice | Calendar the cancellation window 90 days out |
| No response to notice | Vendor stalls | Certified mail + registered agent service |
| Data "retained for legal" | Scans not deleted | Demand certificate of destruction |
| Hardware ownership dispute | Vendor claims equipment | Check contract for lease vs. purchase terms |
| Shared data with other agencies | Scans already shared | Request sharing logs via FOIA |

**Alternatives to full removal:** Some cities downgrade to a "no-sharing" configuration—cameras stay but data isn't shared with other agencies or immigration enforcement. Others switch to fixed vs. mobile units or a competing vendor with clearer termination terms. If you're evaluating replacements, compare deletion SLAs and data-sharing defaults, not just price.

**Cost angle:** ALPR contracts commonly run $2,500–$3,000 per camera per year. A city with 20 cameras is paying $50K–$60K annually—enough that a stalled cancellation is a real budget line, not a paperwork nuisance.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Can a city just unplug Flock cameras without legal risk?
Yes, if the contract term has ended or the city properly terminated and the vendor failed to respond. Keep the hardware intact and documented to avoid property-damage claims—Plymouth's approach of disconnecting rather than destroying is the safer play.

### How long does Flock keep license plate data?
Flock's standard retention is 30 days for most customers, though configurable. After a valid termination, the city should demand written confirmation of deletion—don't assume it happens automatically.

### What if the vendor keeps billing after cancellation?
Dispute the charge in writing, cite your termination notice and non-response record, and escalate to procurement. Auto-renewal clauses are the usual culprit—check whether you cancelled inside the required window.

## Final Verdict

If your city wants out of an ALPR contract, **don't rely on a council vote alone.** Send certified termination notice, document the vendor's non-response, disconnect hardware without destroying it, and demand a certificate of data destruction. Plymouth's police chief showed the endgame—but the paper trail that justifies it has to come first. Residents: file the public records request and put your council on the clock.
