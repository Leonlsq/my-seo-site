## How Do You Actually Pick Your Favourite Laptop in 2026?

The honest answer: "my favourite laptop" is not a product — it's a *fit*. A developer's favourite machine, a gamer's favourite, and a student's favourite share almost no specs. The conversation around the original blog post and its Hacker News thread (128 points, 95 comments) proves the point: people defend wildly different machines because they're optimizing for different workloads. This guide turns that debate into a repeatable decision process so you can name *your* favourite laptop with confidence — and avoid the four-figure mistakes most buyers make.

The direct takeaway up front: **pick your primary workload first, then your display, then your keyboard and battery, and only then compare CPUs.** Most buyers do this in reverse and end up with a fast laptop they hate using.

## Step-by-Step: Choose Your Favourite Laptop in 5 Decisions

### Step 1 — Name Your Primary Workload (and Be Brutally Honest)

Write down what you do 80% of the time. Then match it to a category:

| Workload | Priority Specs | What to Deprioritize |
|---|---|---|
| Coding / dev work | 16–32 GB RAM, great keyboard, 14" 16:10 display | Discrete GPU, 4K panel |
| Gaming | Discrete GPU (VRAM matters), 165 Hz panel, cooling headroom | Ultra-thin chassis, long battery |
| Video / 3D editing | 32 GB+ RAM, color-accurate display (100% sRGB/DCI-P3) | Light weight |
| Student / office | 16 GB RAM, 10+ hr battery, sub-1.5 kg weight | High-end CPU |
| Travel / writing | Keyboard feel, battery, matte screen | Raw benchmark scores |

If you can't pick one, you're a "coding + light gaming" buyer — the most common profile in threads like this one.

### Step 2 — Set Your Display Rules

- **Resolution:** 1920×1200 or 2560×1600 on 14". Skip 4K on laptops under 15" — scaling costs battery for no visible gain.
- **Aspect ratio:** 16:10 or 3:2 for productivity; 16:9 only if gaming is your priority.
- **Panel:** IPS or OLED. OLED wins on contrast but check for burn-in policies if you leave static IDE windows open all day.
- **Refresh rate:** 120 Hz+ is now table stakes and noticeably improves scrolling and cursor feel.

### Step 3 — Lock In RAM and Storage Before You Buy

This is the single biggest regret category, because **soldered RAM cannot be upgraded**:

- **16 GB minimum** for 2026; 32 GB if you run containers, VMs, or a browser with 40+ tabs.
- **512 GB SSD minimum**, 1 TB preferred. Check whether the SSD is a standard M.2 slot (upgradeable) or soldered.
- Verify RAM type: LPDDR5X is fast but soldered; SODIMM slots mean future upgrades.

### Step 4 — Audit the Keyboard, Trackpad, and Ports

You will touch these every day; you'll read benchmark scores once.

- **Keyboard:** Look for ≥1.3 mm key travel and a full-size arrow cluster. If possible, type on a store demo for five minutes.
- **Ports:** Aim for at least two USB-C (Thunderbolt/USB4), one USB-A, and HDMI 2.1. Docking is fine, but dongle life gets old.
- **Webcam and mic:** 1080p minimum in 2026; check for a physical privacy shutter.

### Step 5 — Compare Battery *and* Charging Realistically

Published battery hours are meaningless. Search for real-world reviews measuring **web browsing at 150 nits**. Practical targets:

- Ultraportable: 10+ hours real-world
- 14" all-rounder: 8–10 hours
- Gaming laptop: 4–6 hours (and expect a heavy 200 W+ brick)

Also check **USB-C charging support** — being able to top up from a 65 W GaN charger instead of the OEM brick changes how you travel.

## Common Mistakes, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

### Mistake 1: Buying for Peak Load Instead of Daily Load
A 3 kg gaming laptop for occasional gaming and daily spreadsheets is a bad trade. Consider a lighter laptop plus a cloud gaming subscription or an eGPU-less desktop for heavy sessions.

### Mistake 2: Ignoring Thermal Throttling
Two laptops with the same CPU can differ by 30% in sustained performance. Look for reviews that run **20-minute Cinebench loops**, not single runs. Thin chassis + high-wattage CPU = throttling.

### Mistake 3: Overpaying for the Top SKU
The mid-tier CPU is usually within 10–15% of the flagship for real workloads. Put the savings into RAM and storage instead.

### Alternatives by Budget Tier

- **Budget (<$700):** prioritize 16 GB RAM and an IPS 16:10 panel; accept a mid-tier CPU.
- **Mid-range ($700–$1,300):** the sweet spot — 16–32 GB RAM, 1 TB SSD, 120 Hz display.
- **Premium ($1,300–$2,000+):** buy for chassis, keyboard, display, and support — not benchmark deltas.

### The Repairability Check
Before checkout, search "[model] teardown" and check whether RAM/SSD/battery are user-replaceable, and whether the manufacturer publishes service manuals. A laptop you can service in year four is a laptop you'll still like in year four.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What specs matter most when choosing a favourite laptop?
For most people: 16 GB+ RAM, a 16:10 IPS/OLED display, a comfortable keyboard, and 8+ hours of real battery life. CPU and GPU matter only after these are satisfied for your workload.

### Is 16 GB RAM enough for a laptop in 2026?
Yes for browsing, office work, and light coding. Choose 32 GB if you run virtual machines, containers, large datasets, or heavy multitasking — especially if the RAM is soldered and can't be upgraded later.

### Should I buy a gaming laptop or a thin-and-light?
Choose a gaming laptop only if gaming is a primary use and you accept 4–6 hours of battery and 2+ kg weight. Otherwise, a thin-and-light with strong integrated graphics covers coding, media, and casual gaming better.

### How many hours of battery life should a laptop have?
Target 8–10 hours of real-world web browsing for a daily driver, 10+ for travel-focused ultraportables, and accept 4–6 hours for gaming machines.

## Final Verdict

Your favourite laptop is the one that disappears while you work. Decide in this order — **workload → display → RAM/storage → keyboard and ports → battery** — and you'll narrow any market to two or three candidates in under an hour. If you want a single default recommendation for the most common profile (coding plus light gaming), buy a 14" all-rounder with 32 GB RAM, a 1 TB upgradeable SSD, a 120 Hz 16:10 display, and USB-C charging — and spend the remaining budget on a good external monitor and keyboard rather than a faster chip. That combination will out-last almost any flagship spec sheet.
