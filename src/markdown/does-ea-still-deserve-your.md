## EA Buyout 2026: Sell Your Shares, Hold for the Payout, or Just Stop Buying Games?

The $55 billion Saudi-led take-private of Electronic Arts is the biggest leveraged buyout in gaming history, and it forces two very different decisions on two very different audiences. If you own EA stock, you're staring at a fixed cash-out price and need to know whether selling on the open market now beats waiting for the deal to close. If you're a player, you're looking at a publisher that just paid CEO Andrew Wilson roughly **$105.9M in cash** for his lifetime stock portfolio while shuttering studio after studio — and wondering whether your $70 pre-order is still a rational purchase. This guide breaks down both decisions with concrete numbers, a timeline, and a clear verdict.

For context: that ~$105.9M lifetime cash-out is roughly **132x** his original 2013 base salary, and it exceeds the entire annual marketing budget of many mid-size studios.

## The Studio Graveyard: 13 Years of Closures

The compensation story only matters because of what happened to the studios funding it. A partial timeline of closures and consolidations during Wilson's tenure:

- **2014 — Mythic Entertainment**: Shuttered. Responsible for *Dark Age of Camelot* and *Warhammer Online*.
- **2015 — Maxis (Emeryville)**: Closed. The *SimCity* and *Sims* studio — though EA retained the Maxis name and continued the franchise under other teams.
- **2016 — Visceral Games**: Closed mid-development on a single-player *Star Wars* project.
- **2017 — BioWare Montreal**: Absorbed after *Mass Effect: Andromeda*.
- **2019 — EA mobile studios in Japan/Australia**: Multiple closures tied to mobile restructuring.
- **2023 — Industrial Toys**: Closed after the *Battlefield Mobile* cancellation.
- **2024 — Ridgeline Games**: Closed; *Battlefield* campaign duties shifted to Criterion and Motive.

The pattern is consistent: acquire or inherit a studio, extract the IP, close the team, and retain the brand name. That's the actual product of the last 13 years — not *Mass Effect 5*.

## Should You Sell Your EA Shares? A Decision Framework

If you own EA, the buyout creates a narrow arbitrage window. Here's how to think about it:

1. **Check the deal price vs. current trading price.** In a cash take-private, shares typically trade at a small discount to the offer price until closing — that gap is the market's estimate of deal risk.
2. **Calculate your annualized return.** If the spread is 2% and closing is 6 months out, that's roughly a 4% annualized return — compare that to a money market fund before deciding to hold.
3. **Assess regulatory risk.** A $55B deal involving a sovereign wealth fund will draw CFIUS and EU scrutiny. Any delay extends your holding period and reduces annualized yield.
4. **Decide if you want to be a forced seller.** Once the deal closes, your shares convert to cash whether you like it or not. There is no post-close "EA" to hold.
5. **Tax-plan the exit.** A buyout cash-out is a taxable event. If you've held shares long-term, confirm your cost basis *before* closing so you're not scrambling in April.

**Bottom line for shareholders:** unless the spread is unusually wide, holding to close is a low-return, deal-risk trade. Most retail holders should treat this as a forced exit and plan accordingly.

## Should You Stop Buying EA Games? A Player's Framework

For players, the question is simpler but more emotional. Here's a practical filter:

- **Buy if** the game is a complete, reviewed, single-player release with no live-service dependency (think *Jedi: Survivor*-style titles).
- **Wait for a sale if** the title is a live-service game with a battle pass — the studio closure risk means server support could vanish within 18–24 months.
- **Skip if** the game is a franchise entry from a studio that's already been absorbed — you're buying a brand, not a team.

The key insight: your purchase decision should be about **the specific game's post-launch support risk**, not about Wilson's paycheck. A $70 purchase is not a moral statement about executive compensation — but it *is* a bet on whether the servers stay up.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Is the EA buyout actually happening in 2026?
Yes — the Saudi-led consortium's take-private of EA is the largest gaming acquisition on record at roughly $55B. Shareholders receive cash per share at closing, and EA ceases to trade publicly.

### How much did Andrew Wilson make from the EA buyout?
Approximately **$105.9M in cash** from liquidating his lifetime EA stock portfolio, plus ~$28.5M in fiscal-year stock value paid in cash, on top of a $1.3M base salary and $6.5M performance bonus.

### Will EA games still be made after the buyout?
Yes. EA's publishing and development pipeline continues, but expect further consolidation of mid-size studios and a heavier push toward live-service and mobile monetization to service the debt used in the buyout.

### Should I pre-order EA games in 2026?
Only if the title is a complete single-player release from a stable studio. For live-service titles, wait for reviews and post-launch server stability data before spending.

## Final Verdict

**Shareholders:** Treat the buyout as a forced exit. Unless the deal spread is unusually wide, sell into the market or hold only to capture the final few percent — don't build a thesis around post-close EA.

**Players:** Don't boycott EA out of anger at a CEO's paycheck — that's a $142M story, not a $70 decision. Instead, apply the **studio-risk filter**: buy complete single-player games, wait on live-service titles, and skip anything from a studio that's already been absorbed. That's the rational move, and it's the one that actually protects your wallet.
