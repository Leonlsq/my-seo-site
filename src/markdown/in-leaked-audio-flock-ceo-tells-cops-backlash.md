## Flock Safety Cameras in Your Neighborhood? Here's How to Check and Opt Out

A leaked recording published by 404 Media captured Flock Safety CEO Garrett Langley telling police departments that public backlash against the company "isn't real." Whether or not you buy that framing, the practical question most people actually search for is simpler: **are there Flock cameras near me, what data do they collect, and what can I legally do about it?** This guide walks through how to locate Flock deployments in your area, how the ALPR (automated license plate reader) data pipeline works, and the concrete steps residents have used to push back—from formal data requests to city council action.

## What Flock Cameras Actually Capture (and Where It Goes)

Flock's flagship product is the Falcon, a solar-powered camera that reads license plates and stores them in a searchable database. Understanding the data flow is the first step to any opt-out effort.

| Data Type | Captured? | Default Retention | Who Can Query It |
|---|---|---|---|
| License plate + timestamp | Yes | 30 days (varies by contract) | Local agency + partner agencies |
| Vehicle make/model/color | Yes (Falcon ALPR) | 30 days | Local agency |
| Hotlist alerts (stolen cars) | Yes, real-time | N/A | Local + state + federal partners |
| Facial recognition | No (company states) | N/A | N/A |
| Audio | No | N/A | N/A |

Key facts worth knowing before you act:

- **Retention is contractual, not fixed.** Flock's standard is 30 days, but departments can set shorter windows (some cities have pushed to 7 days or less).
- **"National lookup" is opt-in per agency.** Your local department may share data with hundreds of outside agencies—or none. This is the single most important setting to ask about.
- **Data is not sold to insurers or advertisers** per Flock's public policy, but it *is* accessible to law enforcement partners and, in some jurisdictions, immigration agencies via sharing agreements.

## Step-by-Step: How to Check for Flock Cameras Near You

1. **Search your city's public records.** Submit a public records request (FOIA or your state's equivalent) asking for: current Flock contract, camera locations, data-sharing agreements, and retention policy. Most departments respond within 10–30 days.
2. **Check the Flock transparency portal.** Flock publishes a public "Transparency Portal" per agency at `transparency.flocksafety.com`. Search your city name—it shows audit logs of who searched the data and sharing status.
3. **Use a community map.** OpenStreetMap contributors and local advocacy groups maintain crowd-sourced ALPR maps. Search "[your city] ALPR map" plus "Flock."
4. **Look for the hardware.** Falcon cameras are white, roughly 18 inches tall, solar-panel-topped, and typically mounted on poles near intersections, subdivision entrances, and highway on-ramps.
5. **File a data-deletion request.** If your plate was captured, you can request deletion of your specific records directly from the agency (not Flock). Success rates vary—some departments comply, others cite evidence-retention rules.

## Common Issues, Legal Limits, and Realistic Alternatives

**You cannot "opt out" of being filmed in public.** There is no federal or state law that lets a resident demand exclusion from public-roadway ALPR capture. What you *can* do is change policy:

- **Attend city council meetings.** Contract renewals are the leverage point. Several cities (including examples in California and Texas) have declined renewals after public comment.
- **Request the audit log.** Flock's transparency portal shows every search. If you find searches unrelated to criminal investigations, that's a policy violation worth raising publicly.
- **Ask about the "backlash isn't real" claim.** Per the 404 Media report, the CEO characterized criticism as unrepresentative. Residents can test that claim directly by checking how many public comments their own council received—the record is public.
- **Consider plate-obscuring legality first.** Some states (e.g., California, Texas) restrict plate covers or sprays. Check your state vehicle code before buying anything; fines often exceed the benefit.

**Alternatives if you want neighborhood security without ALPR:** private doorbell cameras (Ring, Nest) stay under homeowner control, and community watch apps like Neighbors don't create searchable plate databases accessible to outside agencies.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Can I request deletion of my license plate from Flock Safety's database?
You must request deletion from the **police agency** that owns the data, not Flock directly. Flock is a data processor; the department is the custodian. File a written request citing your state's records law and ask for confirmation of deletion.

### Does Flock Safety use facial recognition?
Flock states its cameras do not perform facial recognition and capture only vehicle-related data. Independent audits have generally supported the vehicle-only claim, though privacy advocates note the plate database itself enables location tracking over time.

### How long does Flock keep license plate data?
The default retention is 30 days, but it is set per contract. Some agencies configure 7-day retention; others extend it. Your city's contract or public records request will state the exact window.

### Is the Flock CEO's "backlash isn't real" comment accurate?
That's a contested claim. The leaked audio (reported by 404 Media) shows the CEO downplaying criticism to police audiences. Whether backlash is "real" is a matter of public record—council minutes, contract cancellations, and comment counts in your own city are the verifiable evidence.

## Final Verdict

Don't wait for a national controversy to act locally. **Start with a public records request for your city's Flock contract and data-sharing settings**—that single document tells you whether your plate data is siloed or shared with hundreds of agencies. If you disagree with the policy, the contract renewal date is your real deadline, not the news cycle. The leaked audio is a headline; your city's transparency portal and council calendar are where actual change happens.
