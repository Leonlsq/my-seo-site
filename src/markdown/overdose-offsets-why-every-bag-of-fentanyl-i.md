## Why 'Net Life-Positive' Math Collapses: The Real Numbers

Let's take the essay's framing seriously for one paragraph, then dismantle it with data.

The core claim is that a dealer's product kills some users, and those deaths reduce future consumption, creating a "net" benefit. This is a category error. It treats human lives as interchangeable units and ignores every downstream cost. Here's what the actual epidemiology shows:

- **Fentanyl is 50–100x more potent than morphine.** According to the CDC, synthetic opioids (primarily fentanyl) were involved in roughly **70,000+ deaths per year** in the U.S. at the peak of the recent surge.
- **Overdose deaths are not "removals" — they're preventable events.** Naloxone (Narcan) reverses opioid overdose in minutes. Every death is a failure of access, not an inevitability.
- **The "offset" ignores who dies.** The majority of overdose deaths are people in their 20s–40s, often with dependents, jobs, and recovery potential. The economic and social cost is enormous — one study estimated each overdose death costs society over **$1 million** in lost productivity and healthcare.
- **Survivors don't stop using — they use more cautiously or not at all.** The essay assumes a linear relationship between death and reduced demand. Reality is nonlinear: deaths destabilize communities, increase policing costs, and drive users toward even more dangerous supply.

In short: the "net life-positive" claim is a rhetorical sleight of hand. It's the moral equivalent of saying a car manufacturer is "net safety-positive" because crashes reduce the number of drivers on the road.

## Step-by-Step: What Actually Works — A Harm Reduction Playbook

If you or someone you know is using opioids, here's the practical, evidence-based approach. This is the section to bookmark.

1. **Get naloxone — now, not later.** In most U.S. states, you can get Narcan at a pharmacy without a prescription. It's also available free from many harm reduction orgs (search "naloxone near me" or check Next Distro). Keep it where you can reach it in seconds.
2. **Never use alone.** The single biggest risk factor for fatal overdose is using alone. Use the **Never Use Alone hotline** (1-800-484-3731) or a buddy system. If you must use alone, set a timer and tell someone.
3. **Start low, go slow — especially with fentanyl.** Potency varies wildly between batches. A "test dose" of a tiny amount can prevent a fatal error. Fentanyl test strips are legal in many states and cost about $1 each.
4. **Know the signs of overdose.** Pinpoint pupils, slow or stopped breathing, blue lips, unresponsiveness. If you see these, administer naloxone immediately and call 911.
5. **Use the Good Samaritan laws.** Most states protect callers from drug possession charges when reporting an overdose. Know your state's law before you need it.
6. **Consider medication-assisted treatment (MAT).** Buprenorphine (Suboxone) and methadone are proven to reduce overdose deaths by 50%+. They're not "replacing one drug with another" — they're stabilizing a medical condition.

## Common Issues, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

**"But doesn't harm reduction enable drug use?"** No. The evidence consistently shows that harm reduction services *increase* entry into treatment, not drug use. People who use them are more likely to seek help, not less.

**"What about the essay's free speech angle?"** The essay is protected speech, and it's clearly satirical. But satire that mimics serious argument can be misread. If you shared it without context, add a note. If you're a platform hosting it, consider a content warning.

Yes. Synthetic opioids, primarily fentanyl, were involved in over 70,000 U.S. deaths annually at the recent peak. It's the leading cause of death for Americans aged 18–45.
