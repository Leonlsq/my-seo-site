## Is the Elizabeth Holmes Documentary "You Can See Everything" Deceiving You? A Viewer's Fact-Check Guide

A viral analysis of the Elizabeth Holmes documentary *You Can See Everything* argues the film deceives viewers — and the claim is spreading fast on Hacker News and beyond. Before you accept that verdict (or dismiss it), you need a repeatable method for separating documentary editing tricks from documented fact. This guide gives you a practical, source-by-source fact-check workflow you can run in under an hour, plus the specific red flags that make *any* Theranos documentary — pro or con — unreliable by default.

The short answer: treat every Theranos documentary as a **primary source problem, not a truth-telling problem**. The film's chronology, omissions, and framing are what you should audit — not its vibes.

## Step-by-Step: How to Fact-Check the Documentary Yourself

Run this checklist while watching. Pause at each claim and log it.

1. **Timestamp every factual claim.** Keep a running list of dates, dollar figures, and quotes. The Theranos timeline (founded 2003, $9B valuation peak in 2014, first *WSJ* exposé October 2015, Holmes convicted January 2022, sentenced November 2022) is public record — any deviation is a red flag.
2. **Cross-reference against court documents.** The DOJ indictments, SEC civil complaint (March 2018), and trial transcripts are free and searchable. Editorial claims that contradict sworn testimony need scrutiny.
3. **Check the "missing context" pattern.** Documentaries commonly omit: investor due-diligence failures, board members' credentials (e.g., George Shultz, Henry Kissinger), and the role of the *WSJ* reporting by John Carreyrou.
4. **Identify reenactments vs. archival footage.** If audio is layered over B-roll with no on-screen label, it's dramatization — not evidence.
5. **Audit who's interviewed and who isn't.** Count the on-camera sources. A film that excludes prosecutors, lab scientists, or patients is telling one side.
6. **Check the release date against the record.** Anything released before the 2022 sentencing can't account for trial findings — a common deception vector.

### Quick Red-Flag Table

| Red Flag | What It Looks Like | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Unlabeled reenactment | Moody lighting + voiceover | Manufactures emotion, not fact |
| Timeline compression | Events merged across years | Distorts cause and effect |
| One-sided interviews | No regulators/prosecutors | Hides counter-evidence |
| Unattributed statistics | "Sources say..." | Unverifiable claims |
| Selective archival clips | Out-of-context soundbites | Changes meaning |

## Common Issues, Alternatives, and What You Should Know

**The "deceiving" critique itself needs scrutiny.** Viral essays about documentaries often commit the same sin they accuse films of: cherry-picking. Read the original article at readtheprofile.com *and* the 314-comment Hacker News thread, but weight primary documents (SEC filings, court records) above both.

**Better sources to pair with the film:**
- John Carreyrou's *Bad Blood* (the definitive reported account)
- *The Dropout* (podcast and Hulu series — dramatized, clearly labeled)
- SEC v. Theranos complaint (public PDF)
- DOJ press releases on the Holmes and Balwani convictions

**Practical workaround for busy viewers:** watch with a second screen open to the Theranos Wikipedia timeline and the SEC complaint. Log discrepancies in real time. This converts a passive watch into an audit.

**Cost of getting it wrong:** documentaries shape public memory. If you cite a film's framing in a paper, post, or debate, you inherit its errors. Always cite the underlying document, not the documentary.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Is the Elizabeth Holmes documentary "You Can See Everything" accurate?
Accuracy varies by claim. Verify dates, dollar figures, and quotes against court records and the SEC complaint rather than trusting the edit. Treat unlabeled reenactments as dramatization, not evidence.

### Why do people say the documentary is deceiving?
Critics argue it uses selective editing, timeline compression, and one-sided interviews to shape sympathy or villainy. The strongest critiques cite specific omitted facts — check those against primary sources before repeating them.

### What is the best source on the Theranos story?
John Carreyrou's *Bad Blood* and the public SEC and DOJ court filings are the most reliable. Documentaries and dramatizations are secondary and should be cross-checked.

### How do I spot a misleading documentary quickly?
Look for unlabeled reenactments, compressed timelines, missing key interviewees, and unattributed statistics. Any one of these warrants a fact-check before you share claims.

## Final Verdict

Don't outsource your judgment to a documentary — or to a viral takedown of one. Watch *You Can See Everything* with the six-step checklist above, keep the SEC complaint and Carreyrou's reporting open, and cite primary documents in any argument you make. The film may or may not deceive, but **you** control whether you get deceived. Verify first, share second.
