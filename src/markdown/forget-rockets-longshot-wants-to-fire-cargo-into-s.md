## Longshot Space Is Reinventing How We Reach Orbit

For decades, the only way to get anything into space has been to strap it to a controlled explosion and hope the math holds. Rockets are magnificent, but they are also wildly expensive, fuel-hungry, and slow to turn around. Enter Longshot Space, a startup with a gloriously blunt pitch: forget rockets, let us fire cargo into space from a really, really big gun.

That is not a typo, and it is not science fiction. Longshot is building a kinetic launch system that uses a massive ground-based barrel to accelerate payloads to hypersonic speeds before releasing them toward the edge of space. The company is set to make waves at TechCrunch Disrupt 2026, where it will show off the concept and explain why shooting things into orbit could be cheaper than rocketry by an order of magnitude.

The core idea is simple physics dressed up in serious engineering. Instead of carrying fuel with you, you build the energy source into the ground. A long tube, a controlled pressure pulse, and a projectile that exits the muzzle at several kilometers per second. If you can pull that off reliably, the cost per kilogram to orbit drops dramatically, and the launch cadence problem that has bottlenecked the space industry for years starts to dissolve.

## How Longshot's Space Gun Actually Works

The concept borrows from a long lineage of kinetic launch ideas, from Gerald Bull's Project HARP to modern railgun research. Longshot's twist is making it commercially viable and scalable. Here is the breakdown of what makes the system tick.

### The Basic Physics

- A very long, reinforced barrel acts as the accelerator
- A compressed gas or combustion pulse drives a projectile down the tube
- The payload exits at hypersonic velocity, already above much of the atmosphere
- A small onboard kick stage handles final orbital insertion

### Why It Beats Traditional Rockets

- No need to carry massive fuel loads into the sky
- Ground-based energy means cheaper, more repeatable launches
- Higher cadence: fire, reload, fire again
- Ideal for bulk cargo like fuel, water, and raw materials

### The Catch

Kinetic launch is brutal on payloads. The g-forces involved are extreme, which rules out delicate satellites and, obviously, humans. Longshot is targeting rugged cargo, the kind of stuff you need in orbit to build things, refuel stations, and supply future infrastructure. Think water, propellant, structural materials, and shielded electronics.

## Why Is Everyone Talking About This?

Space launch has been dominated by a handful of players, and the cost curve has only recently started bending thanks to reusable rockets. Longshot threatens to bend it again, and in a completely different direction. If firing cargo into space works at scale, it undercuts the economics of every heavy-lift rocket on the market.

The reaction has been a mix of fascination and skepticism. Skeptics point to atmospheric drag, barrel wear, and the engineering nightmare of building a tube kilometers long. Believers point to the fact that the physics is not in question, only the execution. For the space industry, the stakes are enormous: cheap bulk cargo to orbit unlocks orbital manufacturing, large-scale habitats, and deep-space missions that currently die on the launch pad due to cost.

Investors are clearly paying attention. Longshot has attracted funding and a spot on the Disrupt stage, which signals that serious people think this is more than a stunt. Whether it becomes the freight train to orbit or a cautionary tale, it is forcing the industry to reconsider assumptions it has held for sixty years.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Can Longshot's gun launch humans into space?

Almost certainly not, at least not in its current form. The acceleration forces would be lethal to the human body. Longshot is focused on cargo, particularly bulk materials and fuel, which can survive the extreme g-forces.

### Is this the same as a railgun or a space elevator?

It shares DNA with railguns and other kinetic launch concepts, but it is distinct from a space elevator, which would use a tether to lift payloads. Longshot's approach is a ground-based accelerator, closer in spirit to Project HARP than to a tower reaching into the sky.

### When will we see an actual launch?

Longshot is still in development and testing phases. The TechCrunch Disrupt 2026 appearance is about demonstrating progress and attracting partners. Commercial orbital launches are likely years away, but suborbital tests could come sooner.

## Final Thoughts

Longshot Space is betting that the future of getting to orbit is not about bigger rockets but about smarter physics. Firing cargo into space from a gun sounds absurd until you run the numbers, and then it starts to sound inevitable. Rockets will not disappear, but if Longshot delivers even a fraction of its promise, they may no longer be the default. Keep an eye on TechCrunch Disrupt 2026, because this is one pitch that could genuinely change the trajectory of the space economy.
