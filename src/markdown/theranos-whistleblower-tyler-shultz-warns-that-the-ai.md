## Why Tyler Shultz Is Comparing the AI Boom to Theranos

Theranos whistleblower Tyler Shultz just gave investors a blunt warning: the AI boom is "a great opportunity to go deceive people." His logic is simple and uncomfortable. Theranos raised billions on a story nobody could verify, and today companies that have never turned a profit are raising capital at trillion-dollar valuations on claims most investors cannot technically evaluate.

That is not a prediction that every AI startup is a fraud. It is a signal that the *conditions* for deception—opaque technology, charismatic founders, FOMO-driven capital, and a public that cannot check the math—are all present again. So instead of re-reporting the headline, this guide gives you the practical version: **a repeatable due diligence checklist you can run on any AI company, product, or pitch deck before you put money, career, or reputation behind it.**

The core takeaway up front: treat every AI claim like a Theranos blood test. If you cannot independently verify the output, assume it is marketing until proven otherwise.

## The 7-Point AI Due Diligence Checklist (Run This Before You Invest)

Shultz's key lesson from Theranos was that the deception survived because insiders and investors never demanded independent verification. Here is how to demand it yourself.

### 1. Demand a Live, Unscripted Demo
- **Red flag:** demos that are pre-recorded, run on a "special build," or only work on curated inputs.
- **Do this:** bring your own messy, real-world data and ask them to run it live. In Theranos's case, the "Edison" device was reportedly swapped for commercial analyzers during demos—the modern equivalent is a demo secretly calling a human or a bigger model behind the scenes.
- **Verification test:** ask to see logs, latency, and the actual model/API being called.

### 2. Ask for Third-Party Validation, Not Self-Reported Benchmarks
- **Red flag:** accuracy claims ("99.9% accurate") with no external audit, no published methodology, and no named evaluator.
- **Do this:** ask who independently tested it, under what conditions, and whether the results are reproducible. A real AI company will hand you a paper, a public eval, or a sandbox. A hype company will hand you a slide.

### 3. Check the Financial Reality Against the Valuation
The context here matters: unprofitable companies at trillion-dollar valuations. Run a simple sanity table before believing any number.

| Metric to Request | What It Tells You | Theranos Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue vs. burn rate | Whether the business can survive without new raises | Raised ~$700M+ with near-zero real revenue |
| Gross margin | Whether "AI" is a thin wrapper on expensive API calls | Proprietary "secret" tech that was never real |
| Customer concentration | Whether one or two logos are propping up the story | Deals with Walgreens/Safeway masked the gap |
| Unit economics per query | Whether scale makes money or loses more | Cost per test hidden from partners |

### 4. Verify the "Proprietary Tech" Claim
- Ask: **is this a real moat or a prompt wrapper?** Request architecture details under NDA. Legitimate teams will describe their data pipeline, fine-tuning, and evaluation stack.
- Theranos's "proprietary" device was the entire story—and the entire lie. If a company cannot explain *how* its AI works at a high level, that is a structural red flag, not a trade secret.

### 5. Talk to People Who Left
- Ex-employees are the single richest source of truth. Shultz was an insider who spoke up. Seek out former engineers, not just the founder's references.
- Ask specifically: "Was there pressure to overstate results?" and "Did leadership dismiss internal concerns?"

### 6. Check Regulatory and Safety Posture
- In healthcare, finance, and hiring, AI claims carry legal weight. Ask whether outputs are audited, whether there is human-in-the-loop review, and whether regulators have been engaged.
- A company that markets "diagnosis-level" or "decision-grade" AI without audits is repeating the Theranos playbook.

### 7. Stress-Test the Narrative Itself
- **Beware the messianic founder** who claims to be disrupting an industry everyone else supposedly got wrong.
- **Beware secrecy framed as genius.** "We can't tell you how it works" is the oldest trick in the fraud handbook.
- **Beware FOMO language**—"you're early," "last chance," "everyone's in." Urgency is a sales tactic, not evidence.

## Common Traps, Red Flags, and What to Do Instead

- **The "black box" defense:** "Our AI is too advanced to explain." → Legitimate AI can be explained at the level of inputs, outputs, and evaluation. Demand that.
- **Benchmark cherry-picking:** Companies test on easy datasets and hide the hard ones. → Ask for failure cases and error rates, not just wins.
- **The demo-to-production gap:** Works in a demo, breaks at scale. → Ask for production uptime, incident logs, and real customer churn.
- **Valuation anchoring:** A trillion-dollar valuation makes a $50M check feel small. → Anchor on revenue and margins, not on the round size.
- **Advisor logos as proof:** Big-name board members are not verification. → Theranos had a star-studded board and still collapsed. Credentials ≠ evidence.

**What to do instead:** build a one-page "verification memo" for any AI investment or vendor decision. List every claim, who independently verified it, and what evidence exists. If a claim has no third-party evidence, downgrade it to "unverified" and price your risk accordingly.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What did Tyler Shultz actually warn about regarding AI?
Shultz, the Theranos whistleblower, warned that the AI boom creates "a great opportunity to go deceive people"—echoing how Theranos raised billions on unverifiable technology. His point is that hype, secrecy, and FOMO let bad actors raise capital on claims investors cannot technically check.

### How do I tell if an AI startup is exaggerating its technology?
Demand a live, unscripted demo with your own data, ask for independent third-party benchmarks, verify revenue and unit economics, and interview former employees. If the company cannot explain how its AI works at a high level or refuses external validation, treat its claims as unverified marketing.

### Is the AI boom a bubble like Theranos?
Not necessarily—many AI companies have real products and revenue. The Theranos parallel is about *conditions for deception*, not proof that every AI firm is fraudulent. The lesson is to apply the same skepticism Shultz wished investors had used with Theranos: verify outputs independently before trusting the story.

### What was the biggest red flag in the Theranos case?
The refusal to allow independent verification of the technology combined with a charismatic founder and a prestigious board. Investors and partners trusted the narrative instead of demanding reproducible, third-party evidence—exactly the failure mode to guard against in AI investing today.

## Final Verdict

Shultz's warning is not a reason to avoid AI—it is a reason to stop outsourcing your judgment to founders and valuations. **Before you invest in, buy from, or join any AI company, run the 7-point checklist above and write down what you can actually verify.** If a company passes the live demo, third-party validation, financial sanity, and ex-employee tests, it has earned your trust. If it fails even two of them, walk away. The Theranos lesson is simple: the story is free, but verification is what protects you.
